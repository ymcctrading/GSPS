# W.D. Gann & Market Cycles — a plain-English study guide

*Written 2026-09-15, after a research pass through ~20 newly-uploaded
documents (Gann's own private course material, a signed 1954 astrology
letter, several modern interpretive papers, and general cycle-theory
literature). The full technical index with sourcing/copyright detail lives
in `docs/GANN_HISTORICAL_SOURCES.md` — this document is the "explain it to
a person, not a footnote" version, for you to actually read and remember.*

## The one-sentence version

W.D. Gann sold the public a **discipline system** (dressed up as market
wisdom); he taught paying students a **geometry system**; and he never
sold or taught anyone, in writing that survives, the actual **timing
mechanism** he claimed to have — only hints, and a promise that "the
general public is not yet ready for it."

Three layers. Only the first is fully disclosed. The second is now
substantially disclosed (this research pass found it). The third remains
genuinely secret.

## Layer 1: Discipline — what he actually taught everyone

This is the boring-sounding, actually-most-important part, repeated across
every book he ever sold:

- **Risk small.** Never risk more than about 10% of your capital on one
  trade. Divide your capital into 10 parts mentally, even if you don't
  literally split it.
- **Always use a hard stop.** Not a mental one — an actual order. Placed a
  small, specific distance (3 cents, 3 points — a fixed "buffer") beyond
  the level you're trading against.
- **Never rescue a loser by adding to it** ("averaging down"). If you're
  wrong, you're wrong — get out.
- **Add to winners, not losers.** Once a trade is working, you're allowed
  to add size — never on the way down.
- **Move your stop to breakeven once you're ahead.** A real gain should
  never be allowed to turn into a loss.
- **Don't fix a profit target in your head and hope.** Let the trend tell
  you when it's over.
- **Trade both directions equally.** A bear market is just as tradeable as
  a bull market — don't have a bias.

None of this is mystical. It's just: small size, hard stops, cut losers,
ride winners. Gann repeated it in book after book because it's the part he
was actually willing to give away for free.

## Layer 2: Geometry — what he sold to paying students (now substantially uncovered)

This is the part that gets called "Gann technical analysis" today — angles,
squares, hexagons — and the striking finding from this research pass is
that **none of it appears in any book he sold to the general public.** It
only shows up once we found his actual **private paid course** ("The W.D.
Gann Master Stock Market Course") and a **signed 1954 client letter**.
That resolves a question this project's earlier research had left open: is
the Square of Nine really Gann's, or did modern authors invent it in his
name? Answer: it's genuinely his — he just charged for it and never
published it.

**Gann angles.** Draw a line from a swing high or low that rises (or
falls) at a fixed rate — say, 1 point per day. That's a "1×1" angle. Steeper
lines (2 points/day = "2×1") and shallower ones ("1×2") fan out from the
same point. The idea: while price stays above its 1×1 angle in an uptrend,
the trend is intact; a break below it is the first warning sign.

**The Square of Nine.** Picture the numbers 1, 2, 3... written in a spiral,
winding outward from the center like a nautilus shell, instead of in a
straight line. Numbers that land on the same spoke of that spiral (every
45°, 90°, etc.) are, in Gann's framework, "in harmony" with each other —
meaning if a stock's price or a time-count lands on one of those numbers,
it's more likely to be a turning point. It's a real, specific arithmetic
construction (dividing 360° by 9 gives you the spacing), not just a vague
metaphor — but whether it actually *works* is a separate question (see
"What actually holds up," below).

**The Master Square of Twelve.** A second, separate version of the same
idea, built around 12×12=144 instead of 9 — and, per a fuller read of the
same course chapter (2026-09-16), genuinely nested inside the Square of
Nine, not just a look-alike: 144 is 9 full trips around the Square of
Nine's spiral. Gann calls 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 his "Master Numbers" outright,
in a dedicated passage, and gives his own reasoning for each (7 because
it's the most-cited number in the Bible and 7²=49 is "a very important
time period"; 9 because its digits sum to 45; and so on) — read this as
his own stated numerology, not a modern gloss on him.

**Two more Master constructions, found this session.** A "Square of 20"
(20×20=400), applied the same spiral/grid way but anchored to real
calendar dates (the year the New York Stock Exchange was founded, and
separately 1492); and a "Square of 52" (7-day weeks, 52²=2,704), used the
same way for weekly time counts. Neither was previously known to exist.

**The Hexagon Chart.** Now fully read. A different spiral shape from the
Square of Nine — instead of squares, it's rings of a hexagon, and the
numbers that land on the same spoke as the center are 1, 7, 19, 37, 61,
91, 127, 169... (each ring adds a constant 6 more than the last). Gann
explicitly cross-checks this chart against the Square of Nine and the
Master Twelve in the same paragraph, pointing at the same number (66) on
all three and treating that agreement as the actual proof — confluence
across multiple constructions, not any one taken alone, is presented as
the method itself working correctly.

**Retracement levels (eighths and thirds).** After a big move, price often
pulls back to a specific fraction of that move before continuing: 1/2 (the
most important — Gann called it "the centre of gravity"), then 3/8 and
5/8, then 1/3 and 2/3, then 1/4 and 3/4. This maps neatly onto the angle
system above (a 1×1 angle corresponds to the 50% level, a 1×8 angle to
12.5%, and so on) — modern authors explicitly call this "the Gann
equivalent of Fibonacci retracement."

**Numerology.** Gann leaned heavily on specific numbers he considered
sacred, mostly drawn from the Bible: 3, 7, 9, 12, 21. He used them to build
recurring time-count "watch dates" (every 45 days, every 90 days, every 7
weeks — "the death zone" — and so on).

## Layer 3: Timing — the outer shell, now much more visible

Gann insisted, in his own words, across a 45-year career: price and time
are symmetric. A move is "square" when the number of days/weeks elapsed
equals the size of the price move in some unit. He called the underlying
mechanism **"the Law of Vibration"** and, separately, **"cycle theory, or
harmonic analysis."** He stated flatly, in one of his own books, in
capital letters: *"IT IS NOT MY AIM TO EXPLAIN THE CAUSE OF CYCLES. The
general public is not yet ready for it."*

He never explained *why* cycles work — that stayed secret to the end.
But a fuller read of his private course (2026-09-16) found a chapter
literally titled "Master Time Factor and Forecasting by Mathematical
Rules" that gives, in concrete numbers, by far the most complete picture
of *how* he actually used it: a full stack of named cycles (60, 50, 30,
20, 15, 10, 7, 5, 3, 2, and 1 years, each with its own numbered
forecasting rules — "add 10 years to any top to get the next 10-year
top" is a literal quoted rule), scaled down the same way to months,
weeks, and even specific days of the month, plus a real worked example
walking the Dow Jones Industrial Average from 1896 to 1935 month by
month, naming the exact elapsed-time count at every major turn against a
set of 36 fixed angles (fractions of 360°, from 11¼° up to 360°). That's
a real, disclosed, arithmetic system — not a metaphor. What's still
missing is the deeper *why*: he never states what makes 360° the right
unit to divide, or why those particular fractions matter more than
others — he just shows, over and over, that they did. The comparison to
the longest prior move in the same trend, and a stock's own anniversary
as a timing trigger, are both real pieces of this same system, not
separate ideas.

## The astrology, made concrete

This research pass found something genuinely new: a signed, dated 1954
letter from Gann to a paying client, working through an actual coffee
futures trade using astrology. Here's what that actually looked like, in
plain terms:

He converted the price of coffee into a position on the zodiac wheel — for
example, treating every 30 points of price as equal to 1 degree of the
360° circle. Then he looked up where the real planets (Jupiter, Saturn,
Mars, and so on) actually were in the sky on relevant dates, converted
*their* positions into the same price-like units, and looked for
"alignments" — moments where a planet's position (converted to a price)
matched an actual price level, or where two planets formed a notable angle
to each other (90°, 120°, 180° — like a square or opposition in astrology).
He used this to time entries and predict turning dates.

Is this a validated trading technique? No — it's one worked example from
one private letter, is exactly the kind of thing GSPS has an explicit
policy of *not* implementing, and there's no evidence anywhere that it
outperforms chance. But it settles a historical question: Gann really did
practice real astrology, with real planetary positions, using a real
(if arbitrary) price-to-degree conversion — this wasn't just a modern
myth built up around him.

## The other half: general cycle theory (not Gann-specific)

A second batch of new material comes from a different, non-Gann
tradition: Edward Dewey (who ran a mid-1900s "Foundation for the Study of
Cycles") and a modern successor researcher, Ray Tomes. Their core idea:

**Everything that vibrates settles into whole-number ratios with other
things that vibrate near it** — the same reason a guitar string produces a
clean musical note instead of noise. Tomes argues (speculatively, and
this is fringe physics, not mainstream) that economic cycles behave the
same way: a 54-year "long wave" in the economy, for instance, breaks down
into smaller cycles that are exact fractions of it (a third, a sixth, a
ninth), the same way a musical chord breaks into notes.

The genuinely useful piece — independent of whether you buy the physics —
is **Dewey's checklist for telling a real cycle from a coincidence**:

1. Does it show up strongly and consistently (dominance)?
2. Does it recur at a regular, predictable spacing (regularity)?
3. Has it repeated many times, not just two or three (repetition count)?
4. Does the length of each repeat stay roughly constant (constancy)?
5. If something knocks it off-phase temporarily, does it snap back to
   where it "should" be (phase-resumption)?
6. Does the same cycle length show up in *other, related* data series at
   the same time (synchrony)?

That's a genuinely rigorous, non-mystical standard — worth remembering any
time someone (including Gann himself, or this codebase) claims to have
found a "cycle."

## What actually holds up, in this codebase's own testing

This is the part worth remembering above all the historical trivia. GSPS
has actually backtested pieces of this:

- **Old support/resistance levels** (a level tested multiple times, then
  finally broken by a small buffer) is the single most reliably useful
  Gann-derived idea the platform has found — it's the most repeated,
  most concretely-described technique across every one of Gann's own
  books, and it's the best-performing scored criterion in GSPS's own
  backtests.
- **The Square of Nine** — despite being real, genuinely Gann's own
  technique (not a myth) — measured as essentially useless when scored on
  real trades, and was retired from the live scoring system.
- **A few concrete, disclosed Gann rules aren't implemented in GSPS at
  all yet** — the "Rule of Three" (three closes against the trend signals
  reversal), the exact "3-point rule" for confirming a breakout, and the
  fixed annual calendar-date cycle. These are candidates worth adding,
  not because they're historically colorful, but because they're
  concrete and testable.

The practical lesson: **historical authenticity and trading edge are two
different questions.** Something can be 100% genuinely Gann's own,
private, secret technique — and still not work when you actually test it
against real price data. Old-fashioned "did this level hold before"
thinking beat the mystique, every time it's been checked.
