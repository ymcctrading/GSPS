## Ch. 1 — *Speculation a Profitable Profession: A Course of Instructions on Stocks* (pp. 1–44; dated **May 18, 1954**, Gann "nearing my 76th birthday", "52 years' experience")
### Foreword / preparation (pp. 1–3)
- Prepare by looking up **the greatest advance, the greatest decline, and the greatest time period from high or low** a stock has ever had.
- Most profits come in **active, fast-moving** markets (1924–29; Sep 29–Nov 15 1929; Nov 13 1929–Apr 17 1930; Jun 1949–Jun 1950; Jun 13–Jul 14 1950 fast decline; Sep 1953–Apr 1954 fast advance).
- **Keep out of slow trading markets** until a definite change in trend (Nov 1946–Nov 1949 = consolidation/accumulation before a bull market).
- **Cross-currents:** some stocks decline while others advance (1952–54 Studebaker/Chrysler/Celanese down; Vanadium/steels/DuPont/aircraft up).
- Select the leading **group** from the group's average/total value breaking into new highs, then the strongest stock in it (aircraft 1953–54).
- **Low-priced stocks disappoint** — tie up capital; can sit in narrow ranges 5–10+ years then fall. Best: **higher-priced stocks bought after they break into new high ground** with activity.
- New industries / "stocks of the future" (1954): uranium/atomic-energy (Babcock & Wilcox, Bendix, Dow, DuPont, General Dynamics, GE, Newport News, Phillips, Sylvania, Union Carbide, Vanadium, Westinghouse; Vanadium/Molybdenum/General Dynamics best; New Park Mining, Fairchild speculative) — but **buy only when total NYSE value is in uptrend and the group is in uptrend.**
- Outlook May 1954: total NYSE value at record; trend up since 1932, last extreme low Apr 1942, current swing from Jun 1949 ⇒ **expect a decline in the last half of 1954; be cautious buying.**
### Fundamental rules (pp. 3–6)
- **Uptrend = higher bottoms and higher tops; downtrend = lower tops and lower bottoms.** Prices can range for weeks/months/years; after a long time, a break into **new lows ⇒ lower**, an advance **above old tops ⇒ higher**. Hence keep charts far back to know where the stock is between extreme high and extreme low.
- **Charts to keep:** weekly (2–3 yrs back), monthly (≥10 yrs), yearly (5, 10 or 20 yrs); daily (a few months) once a stock becomes very active. ("One good picture is worth 10,000 words.")
- **Follow the main trend:** never too high to buy / too low to sell while trend holds; never short *because* it is high; never take profits *because* price is high; never buy *because* it is low.
- Always a **STOP LOSS**; several small losses are recovered by one large profit; large losses are hard to recover.
- **"Prove all things and hold fast to that which is good"** — buying new highs / selling new lows is most profitable; prove it to yourself.
- **Prolonged advances ending in a runaway market come down faster than they went up** — keep dailies at the end of fast moves and weeklies for the first change in trend.
- **After a rapid advance, the first sharp decline lasts one month to as much as seven weeks** and corrects the overbought position ⇒ secondary advance follows.
- Catching the extremes at the end of **any great time cycle** can yield large money in one year in fast markets.
- **No fixed ideas / fixed prices** for how high/low; don't buy/sell at a price you fix (hope/fear).
- **Protect profits with stops** — profits are capital. "When you are out of the market, the only thing you can lose or miss is an opportunity." Don't exit too soon on a small profit after a move starts; exit quickly when wrong.
- Too soon / too late: wait for a well-defined change in trend, then act. Refers to "45 Years in Wall Street" for many more rules.
- **Hope ruins; fear can save you if you act quickly when wrong** — "the fear of the market is the beginning of wisdom" (Gann's paraphrase of Proverbs).
- You cannot make the market go your way. Small losses are the expense of a successful speculator. **Never average a loss.** Speculation is a profession requiring training like law/medicine.
### Rules for trading in stocks (p.7)
1. **Buy at new high prices or old top levels.**
2. **Buy when prices advance above old low price levels.**
3. **Sell when prices decline below old top levels or high prices.**
4. **Sell at new low price levels.** Safer to wait for **1–2 points beyond** the level, and **still safer to wait for a CLOSE beyond it.**
5. **Closing prices:** in active fast markets use the daily chart and the **close** above highs/below lows; intraday pokes often reverse by the close. "The longer the time period in days, weeks, months or years when prices exceed old highs or break old lows, **the greater the importance** of the change in trend." New highs generally **react back to the old tops = safe buy**; breaks below old lows generally **rally back to the old lows = safe sell.**
6. Stops placed **when you make the trade**, not later.
7. **Never risk more than 10% of trading capital on one trade; after one or two losses reduce the trading unit.**
### The 50% rules (pp. 7–8, 20–21)
- **"The best buying point"**: a decline to **50% of the highest price at which the stock ever sold**; next strongest: **50% between the extreme low and extreme high.**
- **Selling level:** after being considerably below the 50% point, the **first** advance to it is a sell/short level, stop **3 points above** the 50% level (if daily/weekly show topping). Next: 50% between extreme low and high.
- Chrysler worked example: 1946 high 141½ → 50% = 70¾; 1949 low 44 buy point; Jan 1953 high 98½ ("we always expect selling just under 100"); range 44–98½ → 50% = 71¼; Jun 1953 low 70 just below → Aug rally only to 73, closed 71 → short; 1932 low 5 to 141½ → 50% = 73¼ — a **close above 73¼** ⇒ higher; Feb 1954 low 57 — close below 57 ⇒ lower, next old low 44.
- **Strongest / weakest position (p.20):** a stock that sold very high, stayed a long time low, then **crosses 50% of its highest price (or 50% of its extreme range) is very strong**. Breaking **50% of the highest price** = very weak (moderate rally, sometimes a final bottom); breaking **50% of the extreme range** = **weakest possible position**, much lower. Prices often hover at the 50% point for weeks/months; **once exceeded, reverse position.** Celanese 1951 high 58 → broke 29 (50%) Apr 1953 → never 2 pts higher → 16. Studebaker 43½ → broke 21¾ → never rallied 2 → 15.
- **100% advance ⇒ natural reaction** (United Aircraft up >100% from May 1952 in Apr 1954; total NYSE value up >100% from Jun 1949).
- **Round-number resistance:** "we always expect selling just under 100" (p.8).
### Stops (pp. 8–9)
- Stops go **below swing lows / above swing highs**, not merely below the daily low; for bigger moves, above old tops / below old bottoms **on the weekly or monthly chart**.
- Stops **under closing prices** (daily/weekly/monthly) are safer and caught less often than under intraday lows.
- **Stop distance by price level:** ~$10 stock → **1 point** below the close; $20–30 → **2 points**; $90–150 → **3 points** under lows/closing prices (above highs for shorts). ⇒ ≈ 10% → 7–10% → 2–3% — Gann's stop *shrinks as a percentage* as price rises. (GSPS equivalent: `EQUITY_STOP_MIN_PCT/MAX_PCT` band in `lib/strat/levels.ts` — Gann's schedule is price-tiered, not a flat % band; do **not** port points, but note the tiering principle.)
- At extreme high prices in very active markets, the **daily chart gives the first sign** of trend change, later confirmed weekly.
### What to do before a trade (p.9)
Check daily, weekly, monthly, yearly records and **all time periods**; note proximity to old highs/lows of recent weeks or years; **calculate the risk before the trade**; place the stop after entry.
### Timeframe significance (pp. 9–10)
- Crossing a **series of weekly highs/lows** ⇒ change lasting many weeks; **many months' highs/lows** ⇒ several months; **highs/lows several years back** ⇒ nearly always big, long moves. After crossing, expect a **reaction back to about the old high or slightly lower** (and rally back to broken lows). **"The greater the time period when it is exceeded, the greater the move."**
### Rules for stocks (p.10)
- **A.** Bull-market reactions: quick and sharp but **never more than 3–4 weeks** as a general rule ⇒ "stocks are a buy on a reaction of about 1 month in a bull market." Bear-market rallies **3–4 weeks, seldom more than one month**; after sharp severe declines rallies can last **2 months**; likewise a bull reaction can run 2 months after extremes. ⇒ guide to when the main trend is changing.
- **B.** Most money in the shortest time: follow the big money — buy when accumulation is complete and markets are ready to move fast; sell after distribution. Accumulation and distribution **take a long time**; the move after is fast. Markets go **up fastest after a long period** and **down fastest in the last stages** of a long decline.
- Worked examples (pp. 11–14): Boeing (buy new highs Sep 1950 at 37 → 56; Feb 1954 at 57 → 83 in 2 months), Douglas (Oct 1953 new high 74 → 133 in 6 months), Glenn Martin (1952 higher bottom 9; four months in a 2-pt range = support; Feb 1954 crossed 18½ → 25½ on 384 k shares week ending Apr 3 1954 = **public buying + short covering ⇒ reaction due** → reacted to 21¾ = old 1948/1951 highs), United Aircraft (double tops/bottoms; crossed 41½ Oct 1953 → 59½, the highest since Sep 1930 (65) — **64–65 next resistance**), Lockheed, Southern Railway, Vanadium (Mar 1954 new high 47 → 59¾ in 1 month).
- 1954: no manipulation/pools ⇒ moves come from investors; study harder.
### Total value of all NYSE stocks — the "Dollar Trend" (pp. 15–20)
- Gann charts the **monthly total market value** of all NYSE stocks, and of each group (aircraft, 39 steel & iron stocks), published by the NYSE Research Dept. — "a more important trend indicator than the average price" and **"much more accurate and valuable than the Dow-Jones 30 Industrial Average"** (pp. 25, 27).
- Same rules apply to the value line: new highs = buy; broken lows = sell; triple support = buy; one-month declines in uptrends; e.g. Mar 1938 low = **50% of Feb–Mar 1937 high** = buying level; May 1946 high 85 bn, 55 bn above Apr 1942 low ⇒ watch for change; **three months with no gain after a long advance ⇒ reaction due** (Dec 1952–Feb 1953); since Jun 1949 no reaction >5 bn ⇒ next could be ~10 bn (**2× the previous reaction**), which occurred.
- **Resistance projection (p.19):** 1929 high 90 bn **+50% = 135 bn** selling level; also ~325% of the Apr 1944 value ≈ 135 bn.
- **Time (p.19):** Apr 30 1954 = **12 years** from Apr 30 1942 low; Jun 30 1932 → Apr 30 1954 = **262 months**; many changes in trend historically in **April and May** ⇒ watch.
- **How much decline to expect:** use the largest prior declines in value (Feb 1937–Apr 1938: 31.17 bn; May 1946–1949: 21.79 bn) as a guide for the first decline.
### Swings vs. holding (p.21)
Chrysler 1932–1954: range 5→141 = 136 pts, but 10-pt-or-greater swings total **1,458 pts**; catching half = 729 pts ⇒ **trade the swings** (in and out, long and short) rather than hold for dividends.
### Paired long/short & trade logs (pp. 22–24)
- Long Douglas / short Celanese (Jul–Aug 1951 at ~48/52) → $10,600 on $5,200 capital in <3 years.
- Studebaker (short at a lower third top 35 below two tops at 36, stop 37; buy on triple bottom 26; short on break of Feb and May 1952 lows; add on break of Oct–Nov 1952 low; 50% break at 21¾ → 15) and Vanadium (buy after 6 months at 31, stop 29; sell double top 44½/45–45½; buy new highs 41, 47; **sell at 59¾ because Sep 1953 low 30½ + 50% ≈ 61 and record volume — but don't short since main trend still up**) → ~$11,500 on ~$3,500 in 3 years.
- Spread: Vanadium 30½ vs Studebaker 28 (Sep 1953) → 45 pts apart by Apr 1954 ⇒ buy strong, sell weak.
### Low-priced stocks in modern markets (p.24)
Without pools, institutions buy quality (GM, SONJ, AT&T, DuPont, GE, Westinghouse, Douglas, U.S. Steel, Bethlehem, Vanadium); public holds "cats and dogs" in hope ⇒ low-priced stocks will fall faster in the next bear market. **"The day will come when the great time cycle indicates a great depression"** and even blue chips get sold low.
### Aircraft & steel group value worked examples (pp. 25–27)
- Buy the stock in the strongest group position at the group's low (Douglas Apr 1952: declined 66½ → 52; **50% of 43½–66½ = 55; closed April at 57 ⇒ buy, stop 51**). Split stocks move slower (Bell, Lockheed).
- Steel group: lower tops/bottoms each month Sep 1951 → Apr 1952; Bethlehem Sep 1953 at 44½ "not 3 points below the old bottoms," buy, stop 43.
- Vanadium Apr 1954: high 59¾ on very large volume → sell out; must break 54 to indicate lower; close above 60 ⇒ next resistance **67–68**, then **76½ (Mar 1931 high)**.

## Ch. 2 — *Method for Forecasting the Stock Market* + *The Human Body* (pp. 45–67; signed **January 17, 1931**, 78 Wall Street)
### Charts & the weekly third-week rule (p.46)
- Keep **yearly, monthly, weekly and Overnight** charts.
- **Weekly two-week reversal rule:** a stock often reverses the minor trend for **two or three weeks**, and in the **third week does not make a higher top or higher bottom** (or holds several weeks without exceeding the first two weeks' extreme). **It is always safe to buy or sell with a stop 3 points above/below the two-week reverse move.** If the market is going on, it should continue in the **third week**. Same in bull and bear.
- **Overnight Chart** (see Ch. 3): to show a reversal it must go **3 points above the last top or below the last bottom**; it is **based on bottoms** — progressive (higher) bottoms = up, decreasing bottoms = down. "The basis of all movements are calculated from bottoms."
### How to forecast — philosophy (p.46)
- "Every movement in the market is the result of a natural law and of a **Cause which exists long before the Effect** takes place and can be determined years in advance." Future = repetition of the past (Eccl. 1:9 quoted). ⇒ **Cause and Effect** + **Rhythm**, in Gann's own words.
- "**Everything has a major and a minor**"; know the major cycle — most money is made in extreme fluctuations.
- **Great cycle 49–50 years**; "jubilee" years of extreme high/low prices lasting **5–7 years** at the end of the 50-year cycle.
- "7" a **fatal number**, "ruled by the planet Saturn, which brings contractions, depressions and panics"; **7×7 = 49** "the fatal evil year."
### The cycle hierarchy (pp. 47–48) — Gann's own ranking
- **"The most important time cycle is the 20-year cycle, or 240 months,"** and most stocks and averages work closer to it than any other. Its divisions: **5 yrs = ¼ (60 mo); 10 yrs = ½ (120 mo); 15 yrs = ¾ (180 mo); 7½ yrs = 90 mo** ("because 84⅜ [printed 84-5/8] is 15/16 of 90" — i.e. 90-month and its 15/16 point).
- **30-year cycle** "caused by the planet Saturn" (one revolution ≈ 30 yrs); Saturn "rules the products of the earth" ⇒ extremes in commodities ⇒ stocks. Gann repeats: **"The most important cycle of all is the 20-year cycle."**
- **10-year cycle:** extremes every 10 years; "stocks come out remarkably close on each even 10-year cycle."
- **Minor cycles 3 and 6 years; smallest 1 year — change often in the 10th or 11th month.**
- **Rapid markets run 6–7 weeks** then some minor reversal; or continue for months reacting only **two weeks**, resting 2–3 weeks, then resuming; often move right on in the **third week**. **Daily analogue: fast markets move only two days against the main trend and resume on the third day.**
### Proportions (p.48)
- In all movements use angles and calculate the **⅓, ⅔, ¼, ¾ points** of the major or minor move. **"One-half is the most important as it equals the 45° angle, which is the strongest and most fatal. The next in importance is ⅔, which would equal a triangle, or 120°."** ⇒ the retracement ranking **½ > ⅔ > (⅓, ¼, ¾)** is Gann's own (supports `lib/gann/retracement.ts` ranking).
- **"All rules based on Natural Law are applied the same to Time, Space and Volume charts."** (Correspondence — one rule across three dimensions.)
- **Three-to-four-section rule:** "All markets move in three to four sections. **The third or fourth movement up or down marks the culmination.**" A **reverse signal is always given before time expires**; the market may then make 2–3 swings into the same territory, slightly above/below the warning tops/bottoms = accumulation/distribution.
### Time rules for forecasting stocks (pp. 48–50)
- Stocks move in **10-year cycles worked out as 5-year cycles — 5 up, 5 down.**
- **Rule 1:** campaigns don't run more than **3 to 3½ years** one way without a 3–6-month or 1-year counter-move. Many culminate in the **23rd month**; watch months **23–24, 34–35, 41–42, 49–60, 67–72, 84–90**.
- **Rule 2:** bull campaign **5 years = 2 up, 1 down, 2 up**; end in the **59th–60th month** ("always watch for the change in the 59th month").
- **Rule 3:** bear cycle **5 years = 2 down, 1 up, 2 down.**
- **Rule 4/5:** **add 10 years to any top (bottom)** → next top (bottom) of the 10-year cycle, same kind of year, similar fluctuations.
- **Rule 6:** bear campaigns run out in **7-year cycles = 3 + 4 years** from any complete bottom (add 3 → next bottom; add 4 more → 7-year bottom).
- **Rule 7:** from a complete top add **3 → next top; +3 → second top; +4 → third & final top** of a 10-year cycle (3+3+4 = 10).
- **Rule 8:** **top + 5 years → next bottom; bottom + 5 years → next top** (1917 bottom → 1922 top; 1919 top → 1924 bottom). Campaigns never more than **two years in the same direction** (per rules 2–3).
- **Rule 9 — annual forecasts:** the coming year runs close to **10 years back** (1932 ≈ 1902, 1912, 1922); for full work check **30 years back** (1922 forecast: check 1892, 1902, 1912; closest 1912). The 10-year-back and 20-year cycles have most effect. **"Each stock works from its own base or from its own tops and bottoms."**
- **Rule 10 — extreme great cycles:** "There must always be a major and a minor, a lesser and a greater, **a positive and a negative**" ⇒ three moves in 10 years (tops 3, 3, 4 years apart); 5-year = 2 major + 1 minor; **smallest complete cycle 5 years; 10 a complete cycle; 5×10 = 50 greatest cycle** — extremes at the end. **"The number 7 is the basis of time"**: a panic/depression **every 7 years** (1907, 1917…), greater than the 3-year decline; 7×7 = 49 → extremes in the 49th–50th year; extremes also at the end of 30-year cycles. **Always count cycles from tops or bottoms.** ⇒ Polarity stated explicitly by Gann.
- **Rule 11 — monthly:** same as yearly: bottom **+3 months, then +4 (=7)** → minor bottoms/reaction points. Bull reactions may last only **2–3 weeks**, so a market can rise **12 months without breaking a monthly bottom**. Big swings: reactions **≤2 months, the third month up** ("two down and the third up"); bear rallies ≤2 months. **"Most moves run out in six to seven weeks"; 7×7 = 49 days "a fatal turning point."** In a bull year a stock may react 2–3 weeks, rest 3–4 weeks, then advance 6–7 more weeks. Always check whether the **big time limit has run out** before judging a reverse move.
- **Rule 12 — daily:** important daily changes every **7 and 10 days**; natural changes within a month around the **6–7th, 9–10th, 14–15th, 19–20th, 23–24th, 29–31st**. **Watch 30 days from the last top/bottom ("very important"), then 60, 90, 120 days; 180 days (6 months) very important; the 9th and 11th months** from tops/bottoms for minor and often major changes.
- **Timeframe hierarchy of reversals:** daily gives the first short change (runs **7–10 days**), weekly the next (runs **3–7 weeks**), monthly the strongest (**2–3 months or more**).
- **Yearly bottoms rule:** a stock making **higher bottoms each year for five years, then a lower bottom than the previous year** = reversal sign, may mark a long down cycle (mirror for lower tops).
### Instructions for forecasting (pp. 50–51)
1. **"Time is the most important of all factors and not until sufficient time has expired does any big move up or down start. The time factor will overbalance both space and volume. When time is up, space movement will start and big volume will begin… Time rules."** Always consider the annual forecast.
2. **Each stock works out its 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 60-year cycles from its own bottoms and tops**, regardless of others, even in the same group.
3. Monthly reversals after **2–3 months**; the change starts **in the third month**.
4. **Weekly:** declines of 2–3 (sometimes 4) weeks; reversal usually **in the third week**, runs ≤3 weeks against the trend. In a bull market: 2 (possibly 3) weeks' decline; **mid-third-week the trend turns up and closes higher at the end of the third week**; sometimes not until the **fourth week**, closing higher at its end. Reverse for bears.
5. **Rapid advances/declines on big volume run about seven weeks** = culmination moves; watch for top/bottom around the **49th–52nd day**, sometimes the **42nd–45th day**.
- At the end of any big move time must be consumed for accumulation/distribution. After a 7-week decline, 2–3 short sideways weeks then up (= monthly third-month rule).
- Daily counter-moves run **7–10 days**, weekly **3–4 weeks (extreme 6–7)**, monthly **3–4 months** — then resume the main trend; **"as a general rule, in a bear market stocks never rally more than two months. They start breaking in the third month."**
- **Retracement reversal test (p.51):** after extreme advances/declines, **the first time the market reverses over ¼ or ½ of the distance travelled in the last section, consider the trend turned at least for the present.** When time is running out, space shows the reversal by **breaking back over ¼, ⅓ or ½ of the last move.** Never decide the trend has changed without consulting the angles and the stock's position in its cycle.
### Geometric angles (pp. 51–56)
- Draw from bottom "0" upward and from top "T" downward. **First the 45°, then 2×1 on each side**; add 4×1/8×1 (and 1×4/1×8) only for fast moves. Pattern-chart scale **1 point per ⅛ inch**.
- **Rates (monthly chart):** to hold above the 45° a stock must raise its bottoms **1 pt/month**; 8×1 = 8 pts/month; 4×1 = 4; 3×1 = 3; 3×2 = 1½ pts/month (18/yr); **2×1 (the flatter side) = 6 pts/year = ½ pt/month**; flatter angles 4, 3, 2 pts/year; the 6×1-flat angle only 2 pts/yr — a stock that can't hold above such a flat angle is "on the bear side of the square." (The transcription marks left/right and 3×2 vs 2×3 as uncertain.)
- **Angles need not be drawn from far back — calculate them:** bottom 15 in Jan 1900 → 45° rises 1 pt/month → Jan 1930 (~360 mo) … Gann's example gives 120 months ⇒ 135 (text says "20 years later… 10 years would be 120 months… 135 in January 1930"; internal inconsistency in the source — the arithmetic is bottom + months elapsed).
- **Angle degrees on the Pattern Chart:** 3¾°, 7½°, 15°, 18¾°, 26¼° [printed 26½], 30°, 33¾°, 37½°, 45°, 52½°, 56¼°, 60°, 63¾°, 71¼°, 75°, 82½°, 86¼°, 90° (these are 1/24ths and 1/16ths of 90°).
- **Proof by crossing:** 8×1 up from 0 and 8×1 down from 90 cross on the 45 line at 5⅝ over; 4×1 pair crosses at 11¼ — because 45 is ½ of 90, **parallel angles from 0 up and 90 down meet at the "gravity center."**
- **From any bottom two 45° angles** (up and down from the vertical); likewise from any top. Worked: U.S. Steel low 150 on Nov 13 1929 → 45° down 1 pt/month → Nov 1930 low 138 on it (12 months = 12 points); Dec 1930 extreme 134⅜, 2 pts under, but **resting on the 2×1 from the Jan 1927 low 111½**; closed back above the 45°.
- **Break of the 45° (daily/weekly/monthly) = very weak, expect the next angle; regaining it = stronger.** Re-crossing reverses the trend again. **Monthly and weekly angles matter more than daily.**
- **Distance from base:** "the farther away from the base, the more important the change in trend" when an angle is broken or crossed.
- **Each stock works out its own square** by its extreme high/low or the square of its tops: U.S. Rubber total high 143 → move over **143 months** (11 yrs 11 mo) → ends Dec 1931 = change-in-trend point; divide the square into ¼, ½, ¾, ⅓, ⅔.
### Why angles work — the circle (pp. 53–54)
- Strength order: **90° (strongest — vertical/horizontal)**, then **180°**, **270°**, **360°**; then **120° and 240°** (⅓ and ⅔ of the circle — "especially strong for measurements of time"); then **45°, 135°, 225°, 315°**. **Cardinal Cross = 90/180/270/360; Fixed Cross = 45/135/225/315** — "very important for the measurements of time and space and volume."
- 22½° stronger than 11¼°; **67½° = 1½×45** strong on the way to 90; **78¾° = ⅞ of 90** one of the strongest before 90 — **many stocks top/bottom around the 78th–80th week, month or day.**
- **Why ⅛ divisions dominate:** money is divided in ¼, ½, ⅛ ($1 = 4 quarters; old 12½¢ pieces); stock prices were quoted in eighths ⇒ price/time work out closer to eighths than thirds. **Par $100 → degrees: 12½ = 45°, 25 = 90°, 37½ = 135°, 50 = 180°, 62½ = 225°, 75 = 270°, 87½ = 315° [printed 82½], 100 = 360°.** A stock at 50 on the 180th day/week/month is "on the degree of its time angle" (price = time). U.S. Steel Feb 1 1915 low 38 ≈ 37½ (⅜ of 100 = 135°) when Steel was **168 months (14 yrs) old** ≈ 135° angle — "behind time" but strong. Crossing 200 = 2 circles; 261¾ ≈ 262½ = ⅝ of the third hundred = 225°.
- **Master Timing Angles** (referenced, instructions not in this chapter): what happens when each timing angle reaches **24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 90, 120, 135, 157½, 180, 210, 225, 240, 270, 300, 315, 330, 345, 360 months** from incorporation; timing angles #5–9 crossing others; returns to the same degree = "exact measurements of cycles according to moving energy," computed from **U.S. Government astronomers' figures** (i.e. planetary ephemerides) out to 2000. *(Astrological mechanism — see AGENTS.md astrology decision: non-gating only, and only with a cited method.)*
### How to use geometric angles (pp. 55–56)
- "My method… is all based on mathematical points or geometrical angles." **Resistance levels are ⅛, ¼, ½, ¾, ⅓, ⅔… proportional parts of a circle** ⇒ geometric angles.
- Three factors: **price, time, space movements**; fourth: **volume at tops/bottoms**.
- Price 45 meets resistance (= 45°). Breaking a 45° angle anywhere weakens; **more important far from the base.** A stock resting on the 45° **three times** in successive reactions then **breaking it the fourth time** is in an extremely weak position (far from base, much time elapsed).
- Daily charts can break/recover angles; a daily 45° can hold a very long time **except in the final grand rush** at the end of big bull campaigns.
- **Time-and-space balance:** breaking the 45° at price **135 in the 135th month** = strong resistance level + strong angle + strong time angle ⇒ big decline. (Price = time coincidence at a strong degree.)
- Volume: sales increase near tops, decrease near bottoms; long liquidation ends on shrinking volume.
### Volume charting — the "space by volume" method (pp. 56–60)
- **Volume chart:** move one ⅛-inch space per **25,000 shares** (Vanadium) instead of per week ⇒ angles drawn on a **volume-time axis**. Worked Vanadium weekly Sep 1928–Jun 1930 with weekly volumes: breakouts on volume expansions; tops on climactic weeks (238,900; 175,000; 206,000; 258,100 ≈ **"volume almost equalling the total amount of stock outstanding" = plain top**); bottoms on dry-ups (8,800 shares Aug 31 1929 = no stock for sale; 12–19 k at Dec 1929 bottom after low 37½ on Nov 13 1929).
- **Halfway point overshoot allowance:** from 37½ to 143¼, ½ = 90⅜; the decline went **3⅜ beyond**, due to volume/momentum — **"often a stock will go 3¾ points beyond the half-way point and then recover, especially after a big decline. The greater the decline in points, the more allowance… around the main center."** Thin-float stocks make faster, wider moves. ⇒ Gann's own tolerance band around 50% (cf. `lib/gann/entryTrigger.ts` lost-motion; `lib/gann/retracement.ts`).
- **Confluence at 125 (p.58):** ⅔ of the 143½–87 swing = 126¾; ⅞ of the total value 143¼ = 125⅜; and 25 = ¼ of 100 ⇒ **"a very strong resistance level"** (multiple proportional levels coinciding).
- After a two-week decline wiping out half the advance on ~⅓ of the whole advance's volume ⇒ "the decline was too fast according to time" ⇒ rally due (rally expectation **⅓ to ½ of the decline**).
- Dow monthly volume 1921–1930: Jun 1928 → Sep 1929 (194 → 381) never more than **one month's reaction**, never broke **10 points under a previous month's bottom**; Oct 1929 first break of a prior month's low since May 1929 ⇒ trend down. Nov 1929–Apr 1930 = bear-market rally (111 M in April on tiny price gain), rallying up **under the 1929 heavy-volume distribution zone.** Chart scale 2 pts per ⅛ inch **doubles angle values** (2×1 behaves as 45°) — **angle meaning depends on the price-time scale** (a scaling choice Gann makes explicit). 248 = crossing of two angles (from Nov 1929 and from Oct 1919) = important point; Jan 1930 low 246.
### Fast advances/declines & the "square of distance" (pp. 60–62)
- Fast advances reverse sharply before breaking the 67½°/45° on monthly/weekly charts because **the volume chart breaks the angle first** (large volume moves the "space" axis faster than calendar time). ⇒ **volume-time is the leading axis at climaxes.**
- **Square of distance:** after a 100-pt decline in 2–3 months, **"to overcome the square of distance at a low level, it requires 100 months"** — a stock must "square itself" on weekly, monthly and daily charts. **The lower the price at which it crosses the 45°, the stronger; the higher/farther from base when an angle is broken, the weaker.**
- First crossing of the 45° often comes **before** the square of distance is overcome ⇒ secondary reaction rests on the 45° **when the square of distance is reached**, then a greater advance. Mirror at tops: quick decline, then slightly higher or series of slightly lower tops, until the square is overcome at a high level, the 45° breaks and a fast decline follows.
- **Draw angles from each higher bottom** (and each lower top); **the second higher bottom / second lower top is always very important** to draw angles and measure time from.
- **When in doubt, don't trade.** Wait for a break/cross of a resistance level or important angle, especially after a long deadlock; important trend changes show in volume (volume rises as a stock starts from dullness, and when a high-level dull period breaks down). **Important angles cannot be crossed at extreme lows, or broken at highs, until proper time has elapsed ⇒ an angle break usually means time has run out.**

## Ch. 3 — *Method for Trading with the Overnight Chart — Mechanical Stock Trading Method* (pp. 68–116; 91 Wall Street; log to ~Mar 1931)
**"Purely mechanical. You use no judgement but simply follow rules and reverse your position when the Overnight Chart indicates it."** — the most directly codeable system in Gann's corpus.

### Construction of the Overnight Chart (p.68) — algorithm
Input: daily high/low. State: current direction, current chart level, last recorded top/bottom.
1. **While each day makes a higher bottom, move the chart up** (to that day's high — the chart line follows the up-swing).
2. **The first day the low is ≥ ¼ point under the previous day's bottom, move the chart down** to that low, **recording the highest top reached before the turn.**
3. While it makes lower bottoms, keep moving it down.
4. **A higher bottom and a lower top on the same day (inside day) → move it up to that day's top** ("because the Overnight Chart is based on bottoms").
5. **Outside day** (higher top, then lower bottom): first move up to the high, **then** down to the low. Next day a higher bottom → move up to that day's top.
⇒ This is a **one-bar swing chart keyed on the lows** (a ¼-pt filter on the break of the prior low). It differs from `lib/gann/swingChart.ts` (3-day/9-day, close-count based). *Implementation note:* modern equivalent of "¼ point" = a minimum tick/ATR filter; Gann's 3-point reversal (Ch. 2 p.46) is the larger-swing variant.

### Trading rules (pp. 68–71)
- Use only the **half-way point** ("gravity center": extreme low + extreme high of the last move ÷ 2) plus the Overnight Chart in the worked log: **buy/sell when the half-way point is reached, 1-point stop.** Where no resistance levels exist (new highs), just follow the Overnight Chart. Watch daily highs/lows near Permanent Resistance Levels.
- **Rule 1:** buy/sell **double or triple tops/bottoms** with a stop **1 point** beyond (a 3-pt stop often makes more but is caught less… Gann uses 1 pt; when caught, reverse). **Greatest moves start from triple tops/bottoms several weeks or months apart**; ones only days apart mean smaller moves.
- **Rule 2:** **the 4th time at the same top/bottom it nearly always goes through** — use a 1-pt stop when trading the 4th test.
- **Rule 3:** Overnight tops/bottoms **at ½, ⅔, ¾ resistance levels** → buy/sell with a stop 1–2 (generally 1) point beyond the exact level.
- **Rule 4:** **when the stop is caught the Overnight Chart has reversed — reverse position and "double up"** (i.e. the stop order both exits and opens the opposite position of the same unit; "sell out longs… go short the same amount"). Exception: don't reverse where there's no nearby second top/bottom to place the new stop against. Stops generally **1 point beyond a previous top/bottom.**
- **Rule 5 — pyramiding:** add **half the trading unit every 3–5 points** (distance by activity and resistance levels broken); **3rd lot = ½ of 2nd, 4th = ½ of 3rd…** — largest risk first. With a large profit in a sharp move: trail the stop **5 points from the day's extreme**; out on the first 5-pt reverse move.
- **Rule 6:** very active, fast stocks at high levels → wait for an actual Overnight Chart change (break of a previous low/high) before reversing.
- **Rule 7:** after any big move, reverse on an Overnight change ("if it is time to sell out longs, it is also time to go short"). In panics (Oct–Nov 1929) with big pyramid profits, trail **~10 points** from the market, then **~5 points** above the low after a severe decline — **don't wait for the Overnight Chart** in such fast markets. Also stop at **important half-way points** (U.S. Steel 150 = ½ of 38→261¾: cover and buy at 150 with stop 149, or 147 by the 3-point rule).
- **Rule 8:** when an active stock **closes for 3 days, 3 weeks or 3 months around the same price**, then the trend changes, it usually goes a considerable distance in the new direction (supplementary, not required for the Overnight method).
- **Rule 9 — "3 full points":** in very weak/strong markets watch for the **first 3-full-point move from any extreme**, counted on **even figures** (low 99½ → needs 103, not 102½; high 150⅞ → reaction to 147 counts, 147¼ does not) ⇒ minor trend reversing.
- **Risk:** never risk >3 points on an initial trade; thereafter 1-pt Overnight stops; **$3,000 per 100 shares** ($300 → 10-share lots, ≤$30 risk); don't raise the unit until a loss would be ≤10% of capital; **pyramid stops set so a full stop-out loses ≤10% of capital.** "Use as little human judgment as possible… **You must have machine-like action.**"
- Gann states he deliberately makes known-losing trades in the log "just to demonstrate that the Method will work over a long period of years."

### U.S. Steel worked log, Feb 1915 → Jun 1930 (pp. 72–116) — characteristics
- Start **$3,000, 100 shares** (Feb 1 1915 low 38; entry on the first **3-point rally** to 41½, buy 41). Trading unit stepped **100 → 200 → 300 → 700 → 1,000 → 1,200 → 1,500 → 2,000 → 5,000** as capital grew (≈ $3,000 of capital per 100 shares, then "ultra conservative" once large — at $121 k Gann notes **ten consecutive 3-pt losses would still leave half the capital**).
- Claimed result: **$5,957,511 net by Jun 7 1930** (after an explicit commission/tax **"ERROR" correction of $162,191** he inserts himself), ~$6.02 M by Jun 30 1930. Treat as **illustrative, hindsight paper trading** — not evidence of expectancy (he chooses the half-way levels and some discretionary exits).
- Recurrent decision points in the log (each a codeable rule): (a) **1 pt beyond the last Overnight bottom/top** as the reversing stop; (b) **stop 1–2 pts under two/three bottoms close together**; (c) **cover and reverse at the ½ point of the last swing** with a 1-pt (or 3-pt) stop (e.g. 54½ = ½ of 60¾–48¼; 67⅞; 82⅛; 107¾; 114⅞; 97; 96⅝; 88⅝; 106⅜; 104⅛; 205¾; 234⅛); (d) **new-high breakouts after long ranges** (Aug 1916: +1 pt into new high after **nine months between 89½ and 79¾**) start new pyramids; (e) **round-number resistance 100** ("can't go thru 100… sell at 99"; later buy at 100½ once through); **120, 150 as resistance levels**; (f) **the long-term halves**: 150 = ½ of 38→261¾ (and 1928 move start 149¾ ⇒ "strongest support"), 166⅛ = ½ of 70½ (1921) → 261¾, 135 = ½ of 8⅜ (life low) → 261¾ "half of the life fluctuation", 169 = **⅜** of 198¾→151⅝ (a ⅜ level used as an exit); (g) **"two years from the low"** — Feb 1 1917 is exactly 2 years from Feb 1 1915 low 38 (time anniversary noted in the log); (h) panic days: **pull stops 5 pts from the low**; **fast double-pyramid: stop 10 pts under each day's high** (Aug–Sep 1929: sold all longs at 251¾ on Sep 3 1929 — the top day was Sep 3, high 261¾); (i) **"failing to get 3 points above the ½ point"** → exit (Feb 1931, 152¼ vs ½ point); (j) crossing 152–153 → "very strong position."

### Implementation relevance for GSPS
- The **Overnight Chart** is a distinct Gann swing definition (bar-low keyed, ¼-pt filter, inside/outside-day handling) not present in `lib/gann/`. It is a candidate *second, finer swing definition* next to `swingChart.ts` — and it is the swing definition Gann himself used for **stops** (1 pt beyond the last Overnight bottom). `lib/strat/levels.ts#nearestStructuralStop` uses structural pivots; the Overnight rule is the Gann-specific version.
- **Stop-and-reverse** is Gann's own default in this method; GSPS does not reverse. Relevant to Strategy/automation design only if a "Gann mechanical" mode is ever wanted — note that AGENTS.md says Gann is the default *verdict*, and this is literally Gann's mechanical system.
- **Pyramiding with halving units** and **trailing 5/10 pts from the extreme in fast markets** — no GSPS equivalent.
- **Half-way-point reversal trades with 1-pt stops** — `lib/gann/retracement.ts` supplies the level; the *trade rule* (enter at the ½ of the last swing, stop 1 pt beyond) is not in GSPS entries.

## Ch. 4 — *The Basis of My Forecasting Method — Geometric Angles* (pp. 117–156; lesson pages GA-1…GA-32; signed **November 1935**)
### Philosophy (GA-1)
- "Mathematics is the only exact science." Emerson: "God does indeed geometrize"; Pythagoras: "Before God was numbers" — vibration of numbers. Arithmetic does only two things: **increase or decrease.**
- **"Everything in nature is male and female, white and black, harmony or inharmony, right and left. The market moves only two ways, up and down."** (Gender + Polarity in Gann's own words.) Three dimensions; three figures — **circle, square, triangle**; the 360° circle measures Time and Price; three angle kinds — vertical, horizontal, diagonal; "we use the square of odd and even numbers to get not only the proof of market movements, but **the cause**."
### Charts to keep (GA-2/3) — including two not in other books
- **Yearly** high-low; **Monthly** high-low — "the most important chart of all in determining the main trend" (1 pt per ⅛ inch); **Weekly** (½ pt/space below 50; 1 pt/space above 100 when active); **Semi-weekly / 3-day chart** — Mon open→Wed close, Thu open→Sat close (half-weeks) — "very important"; **Weekly moving-average / mean point = (weekly high + weekly low)/2** plotted as a dot; **Daily** (market days only, no gaps for holidays/Sundays, but **carry calendar days every two weeks** because time counts for changes (30, 60, 80?, 120, 135… days) are in **calendar days**; a trading-day angle and a calendar-day count coinciding = "doubly important point").
  - ⇒ **Gann defines a "moving average" as the (H+L)/2 mean of each period** and says geometric angles are the *regular* version of it ("geometrical angles, which are really moving-averages, move up or down at a uniform rate"). Relevant to AGENTS.md F2.5 (SMA removal): Gann's own "moving average" is **not an N-period SMA** — it's the per-period midpoint, and his real trend line is the fixed-rate angle.
### Why angles (GA-3/4)
- "Geometrical angles measure accurately **space, time, volume and price**." 35 years of research. Angles **self-correct counting errors** (a 45° drawn down from 120 must hit 0 at 120 across). Angles keep the position in front of you; they **are** moving-average trend lines at a uniform rate.
### Construction: the square and the Pattern Chart (GA-4/5)
- Square of 28: vertical + horizontal mid-lines + both diagonals ⇒ 2, 4, 8 equal parts, 8 triangles; **the strongest support/resistance is the center where four angles cross.** A top of 28 → 28×28 square = **squaring price by time** (28 days/weeks/months squares a 28-point range).
- **Pattern Chart = Square of 90** with angles 3¾, 7½, 15, 18¾, 26¼, 30, 33¾, 37½, 45, 52½, 56¼, 60, 63¾, 71¼, 75, 82½, 86¼, 90°; count spaces (8×8 to the inch) rather than measure.
### Angles from a low (Form 2) — bull side (GA-5…GA-7)
1. **45° (1×1) — "the first and always most important"**, 1 pt per day/week/month. Above it = strong. **Buy each time it rests on the 45°, stop 1–3 pts under (never >3)**; normally **1 pt** under except near low levels/early bull markets. **Broken by 1 point ⇒ trend changed at least temporarily.** "You can beat the market by trading against the 45° angle alone."
2. **2×1** — 2 pts/period, "63¾°" in his Pattern-Chart convention; stronger than the 45°; **"No matter what angle the stock breaks under, it indicates a decline to the next angle below it."**
3. **4×1** (75°), 4. **8×1** (82½°) — strongest position; 5. **16×1** (86¼°) — only in 1929-type markets.
- **3×1** (71¼°, drawn in green) — useful after prolonged advances, on monthly/weekly.
- All important angles lie between 0 and 90° (135, 225, 315 are 45° in other quadrants). **90/8 → the ⅛ angles; 90/3 → 30° and 60°** for time and resistance.
- **When to start angles from a bottom:** daily — after a **3-day rally** with higher bottoms and tops, draw the 45° and 2×1; weekly — after a 3-week+ decline and a 2-week+ rally.
### Bear side of the square (Form 3) — after the 45° from the bottom is broken (GA-8/9)
- First support: **2×1 bear-side** (½ pt/period, 26¼°) — often rests there, sometimes long; then **4×1** (¼ pt, 15°), then **8×1** (⅛ pt, 7½°) — "often a very strong support angle… may make final bottom from this angle" after prolonged declines (monthly/weekly); **16×1** (1/16 pt/month, 3¾°) on monthly after long time.
- **3×1 bear-side (⅓ pt/period, 18¾°, "drawn in red ink") — "a very important angle… I strongly advise using it at all times and keeping it up on monthly charts from any important bottom."**
### Angles from tops (Forms 4–5) (GA-9…GA-11)
- Start after a top has declined ~**3 days / 3 weeks / 3 months** breaking previous bottoms. Draw the **45° down** (below it = weakest, bear market), and the faster 2×1, 4×1, 8×1 down (**under 8×1 = weakest possible**). Crossing the 2×1-down = better rally; crossing the 45°-down after a prolonged decline = may be changing to bull; then draw the slower down-angles (2×1 = ½ pt, 4×1 = ¼ pt, 8×1 = ⅛ pt, **3×1 = ⅓ pt** "important after prolonged declines").
- Always weigh **points up from the bottom and down from the top** together.
### Double/triple bottoms, parallels, zero-angles (GA-12…GA-16)
- **Angles from double/triple bottoms:** 45° from the first bottom and 2×1 from the second — **their crossing = important change point** (Form 6: crossings at 48, 53½, 58). Tops/bottoms need not be exact.
- **Parallel angles:** 45° up from the bottom and 45° up from the first top form a channel; a stock often works up for a whole campaign between them; if far apart, a middle 45° is support. Parallels also form between 2×1 or 4×1 lines (slow stocks).
- **Angles from "0":** from the date of each bottom, draw a 45° starting at **price zero** — it reaches the bottom's price after as many periods as the bottom price (low 20 → 20 days/weeks/months). It becomes support after the angles from the actual bottom are broken; **breaking the 45° from "0" = weakest possible position**; it "proves when **price and time are balancing** or when the stock is squaring out from its bottom." Do the same from the **date of a top** ("last strong support"). Carry "0" angles from 1st/2nd/3rd higher bottoms and lower tops with much time between — especially weekly/monthly. "You could not locate this support point in any other way."
- **45° from a top down to 0 and back up again**; also from a bottom down to 0 and up; **start 0-angles when a major time cycle runs out**: U.S. Steel 1904 low → May 1924 = 20-year cycle end (Gann writes "120 months" — a slip; 20 yrs = 240), top 109 against a 45° from "0" started at the Feb 1915 bottom; 0-angle from May 1924 → 84 in May 1931 (**7-year = 84-month** cycle) — Steel low 83⅛ Jun 1931.
- **Two 45°s from the same bottom (up and down)** separate at **2 pts per period** ⇒ the potential open break is wide when late: U.S. Steel 111¼ (Jan 1927) — 45 months later the two angles were 90 pts apart ⇒ "wide open break" possible (Dec 1931 broke the down-45°; Jun 1932 low 21¼).
- **Price-time balance example:** 45° from "0" at the Oct 1907 bottom 21⅞ crossed **262 in Sep 1929** — Steel topped at **261¾** — "in 262 months… one point per month… Time was up."
- **Top-to-top angles (Chart 9):** 45° from the first top in new-high territory as resistance (e.g. 60 → 74 top → 64 → 90 at month 22 = 16 pts above 74 in 16 months = time equals price above the first top). Breaking the bottom-45° at a high level (24 pts up) = weak. After new highs, a decline to the old top (74) is support **unless broken by 3 pts**.
- **Bottom of the first sharp decline** after a top: draw the 45° **down** from it (squaring out time from that bottom = place to buy a rally; can dip slightly under and hold). **After a sharp break of 2–3 days/weeks/months, a rally, then a break under the first sharp break's low ⇒ main trend down** (mirror: a first sharp recovery's high crossed later ⇒ higher).
- **Last swing:** draw 2×1 and 45° from the start of the final run; breaking the 2×1 = trend turned; the decline resting on the 45° from the top = time and price squared → buy for a rally (stop 2–3 under). Dow Mar 9 1932 top 90 → Jul 8 low 41: **the 2×1 down from 90 crossed 50 in the week ending Jul 30 1932 — first definite sign the trend turned up.** Half-way point of the last big swing (119½ → 40½) = 80; crossing it the second time → 119, never back to 81 until 149½.
- **Momentum crosses and falls back = weakness; fast drops under angles and quick recovery = strength/turn.**
- **Most important points to draw from** (Dow example): extreme high (Sep 3 1929), first sharp-decline bottom (Nov 13 1929), bear-market big rally / second lower top (Apr 17 1930), extreme low (Jul 8 1932), first sharp advance top (Sep 8 1932), **second higher bottom** (Feb–Mar 1933).
### Sections of market campaigns (GA-20/21)
- Campaigns move in sections: advance several weeks/months, **halt several weeks/months in a 5–20-pt range**, resume above the first section's high; **markets often culminate at the end of the third section**; if the third top is crossed, a **fourth section — "generally marks a culmination and a reversal for a bigger decline."** Chrysler 1935: 31 → (1) 49¾ → 41¼ → (2) 62¾ → 57½ → (3) 74 → 68 → (4) 90, then **5 weeks in a 6-pt range = distribution** → down. **Bear-market rallies may make only one section (extreme: two).**
### Strength/weakness by angle position (GA-21…GA-23)
- Monthly/weekly angles > daily. Distance from base matters.
- **Weakest:** distribution complete + broke the 45° from an important bottom (weekly/monthly); or broke the **half-way point** between an important top and bottom; the longer the time and higher the price, the weaker (e.g. at 150, only 25 off the top, breaking the 45° from the low = very weak because it "already squared out the time period with price"). Weakness develops at the ¾, ⅔, ½ breaks; **first weakness = breaking the first important angle up from the last bottom of the final run.**
- **Strongest:** holding above the acute angles; **"the stocks which have the biggest advances are those that always hold above the angle of 2×1 on the monthly chart"** — "rest 10 or 15 times on the 2×1 and never break it until they have advanced 100 points or more" ("stays ahead of time… within the square of time"). Also: advancing above the prior move's half-way point and then holding it on reaction (= resting on a 45°).
- **"The cinch buying point is when a stock rests on a 45° angle"** (stop below); also the **half-way point** (stop under); in uptrends the **weekly/monthly 2×1**.
- Regaining/losing angles flips the trend again. A stock can be **strong from the bottom but weak from the top** (short when it rallies into the 45°/2×1 *down* from the top until it crosses them), or strong from the top but weak from the bottom.
- **45° from the extreme top crossed ⇒ major move.** Dow: 386 top (Sep 3 1929); week of Jan 12 1935 = 279 weeks ⇒ 45° at 386−279 = **107**; Dow 106½ that week, reacted to 100, crossed at 103 (Feb 16 1935), hit 108 (45° from Sep 1934 low 85½ + 2×1 from Jul 1932) → reacted to 96 (Mar 18 1935) resting on the 45° from the 1929 top **and** where a **3×1 (⅓ pt/week) from "0" at Sep 1929** crossed it → new highs. Watch when the 45° from 386 reaches **0 — 386 weeks = late January 1937.**
- **Semi-weekly chart** gives the trend change **2–3 days before the weekly**; better than the daily in narrow markets.
### Newly listed stocks (GA-24)
- No pivots yet → use a **square of 90** (¼ circle) with natural angles from the opening price; or a square matched to the opening price: <22½ → square of 22½; 22½–45 → 45; 45–67½ → 67½; 90–135 → 135 (or a second 90 from 90–180). Breaking natural angles from "0" = breaking angles from a bottom; crossing angles from "90" down = crossing angles from a top.
### Calculating, 3×2, latitude & longitude (GA-24/25)
- Compute crossings instead of drawing (bottom 15 Jan 1900 → 45° at 135 in Jan 1910 = +120 months).
- **3×2** (¾ pt/month, "8 points in 12 months") — seldom used; shows position between widely spread angles.
- **Latitude = price** (horizontal lines at natural angle-levels 11¼, 22½, 33¾, 45, 56¼, 67½, 78¾, 90, 101¼, 112½, 120…); **longitude = time** (vertical lines at the same counts from each top/bottom). **"If the price of a stock at 60 comes out on the 60th day, week or month, it will meet strong resistance because it has reached the square of price with time."** Square of 90 advised on weekly/monthly; start from any pivot or natural points 90, 135, 180; always square the extreme low, high, and 2nd/3rd lower tops/higher bottoms with time.
### Time-count bookkeeping rules (GA-25…GA-27) — codeable conventions
- **Counting from a bottom:** the bottom month belongs to the old down-move; **count the first month up as 1** (numbering every 4 squares per half-inch). From a top: **the top month is 0**, next is 1. Verify angles arithmetically (e.g. 75-pt decline in 40 periods → 2×1-down is 80 below the top).
- **Most important monthly count origins:** the **extreme life low**, and the **date of incorporation / first NYSE trading**; next, a **2nd or 3rd higher bottom — but only once the market has held/advanced 3–4 months** from it. U.S. Steel: incorporated Feb 25 1901 → **Feb 1931 = 360 months (30 yrs) → restart at 0** ("the second cycle or circle of 360°"); extreme low 8⅜ May 14 1904 → 30-yr cycle ends May 1934; 21⅞ Oct 1907 first higher bottom; Feb 1915 third higher bottom. Tops: Apr 1901, Oct 1909, May 1917, **Sep 1929 (most important)**, Apr 1930 (secondary top).
- **Retire an origin when its top/bottom is exceeded by 3 points** (except to project 3, 5, 7, 10, 20-year cycles). **Tops that stay uncrossed longest are most important**; after the extreme high is crossed, the next secondary (lower) top becomes the key origin.
### Squaring the price range with time (GA-28…GA-30) — "one of the most important and valuable discoveries that I have ever made"
- **Definition:** equal number of points and time periods — e.g. up 24 pts in 24 days ⇒ at the 45° ⇒ **watch for an important change**; to stay strong it must stay above the 45°.
- **Reactions are the squaring out of minor time periods; big advances/declines the squaring of major ones.** Check weekly/monthly while squaring on the daily.
- **Squaring a trading range (Form 12):** range 48–60 (12 pts) — run the 45° from the bottom to the top of the range, then back down to the bottom, repeatedly, while price stays in the range; **"every time the 45° reaches the top or bottom of this range there is some important change in trend."** 2×1s on each side also divide it. On breakout, restart from the new bottom / last bottom in the range.
- **Three ways to square:** (1) **the range** (range 25 ⇒ 25 periods, repeating while in the range); (2) **the low price** (bottom 25 ⇒ 25 days/weeks/months; watch the **3rd, 4th, 7th and 9th squares** of the low-price period); (3) **the high price** (top 50 ⇒ 50 periods). **Dow top 386 → 386 calendar days → Sep 23 1930; again Oct 14 1931, Nov 4 1932, Nov 25 1933, Dec 16 1934, Jan 6 1936** — "important changes in trend occurred on the daily chart." For very high tops, divide the price into **8 equal time periods** and watch ¼, ⅓, ½, ¾ — most of all **time = price**. Cross-check squares from the opposite extreme (a top squaring while in the 2nd–3rd square from the bottom = double indication).
- **Weekly square of 52 × 52** (52 weeks; low 50 → square top 102); watch **13, 26, 39 weeks**.
- **Monthly:** **square of 12 (144)**; changes often on **even 12-month periods** from a top/bottom; price resistance at **24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 96, 108…**; price 135 on month 135 breaking a 45° = time & space balancing ⇒ big decline.
- **Price ahead of time:** first cross of the 45° before the square is complete → secondary reaction rests on the 45° when the square of distance is reached → then the greater advance (mirror at tops; breaking the 45° the 4th time far from base = extremely weak).
### Strongest angles for time & price (GA-31/32) — repeated from Ch. 2 with additions
- 90, 180, 270, 360; **270 months = 22½ years = ½ of 45**; 120 and 240 (⅓, ⅔ — "especially strong for measurements of time"); 45, 135, 225, 315; Cardinal & Fixed crosses; 22½ > 11¼; 67½; **78¾ (⅞ of 90) — tops/bottoms around the 78th–80th day/week/month; "don't overlook 84 months or 7 years, a strong time cycle."** ⅛-division of $1; par-100 → degrees table (printed 82½ for 315° — arithmetic gives 87½).

In Gann's own 1935 text the phrase **"Master Time Period" is attached to the 60-year Great Cycle**; the 20-year is "one of the most important" (in the 1931 lesson, Ch. 2, it was "the most important time cycle"). ### Rules for future cycles (F-3/F-4) — 1935 restatement (differences from 1931 noted)
1. Bull campaign **generally** 5 yrs (2 up, 1 down, 2 up); end in the **59th–60th month**.
2. Bear cycle **often** 5 yrs down (2 down, 1 up, 2 down).
3. Campaigns **seldom** exceed 3–3½ years without a 3–6-month or 1-year counter-move **"except at the end of Major Cycles, like 1869 and 1929"**; culminations in the **23rd, 24th, 27th or 30th month**, extreme campaigns **34th–35th or 41st–42nd**.
4–5. **Top + 10 yrs → top; bottom + 10 → bottom** (same kind of year).
6. **Bottom + 3 → bottom; + 4 more → 7-yr bottom** (1914 → 1917 panic low → 1921 depression low).
7. **Top + 3 → top, + 3 → third top, + 4 → final top of the 10-yr cycle**; watch the **27th, 34th and 42nd month** for early reversals.
8. **Top + 5 → bottom; bottom + 5 → top** (1917 → 1922 minor top; 1919 → 1924 bottom; 1917 + 7 = 1924; 1924 + 5 = 1929 top).
### Monthly / weekly / daily forecasting rules (F-4/F-5) — 1935 wording
- Monthly: bottom + 3, + 4 (=7) months → minor bottoms/reaction points; big upswings react ≤2 months (third month up); extreme markets react only 2–3 weeks ⇒ can rise 12 months without breaking a monthly bottom; bull minor trend may reverse **3–4 months**, bear rallies 3–4 months but **"as a general rule, stocks never rally more than 2 months in a bear market"**.
- Weekly: bull reactions 2–3 (possibly 4) weeks; turn up **mid-third week, close higher at the end of the third week** (or the fourth). **Rapid big-volume moves run 6–7 weeks; in 1929-type markets 13–15 weeks (¼ year)** = culminations. **49–52nd day** (7×7) top/bottom; sometimes **42nd–45th day ("45 days is ⅛ of a year")**; **culminations at 90–98 days**. After a 7-week decline, 2–3 sideways weeks, then up.
- **Trading-range rule (F-5):** after a top, 2–3-week reaction, 2–3-week rally not above the top, then a range of weeks: buy near the range low / sell near the high with 1–3-pt stops — **"a better plan would be to wait… then buy when it crosses the highest point or sell when it breaks the lowest point of that trading range."**
- Daily: fast markets move **2 days against the trend, resume on the 3rd**; daily reversals run 7–10 days. **Natural monthly change dates: 6–7th, 9–10th, 14–15th, 19–20th, 23–24th, 29–31st.** Watch **30 days** from the last top/bottom, then **60, 90, 120, 180 (very important), 270 and 330 days**.
### Calendar windows (F-5/F-6)
- **January 2–7 and 15–21:** note the high and low; **until these are crossed/broken, consider the trend up/down**; an early-January low often holds until July/August or all year; an early-January high often is the year's high until after July/August. (U.S. Steel Jan 2 1930 low 166 = ½ of 1921–29; broken ⇒ lower.)
- **July 3–7 and 20–27:** dividend month; changes **180 days from January** tops/bottoms (Jul 8 1932 low, Jul 17 1933 high, Jul 26 1934 low).
### Dividing the year (F-6)
- ÷2 = 6 months / 26 weeks (180°); ÷4 = 3 months / 13 weeks (90°); ÷3 = 4 months / **17⅓ weeks** (120°); ÷8 = 45 days / **6½ weeks — "why the 7th week is always so important"**; ÷16 = **22½ days ≈ 3 weeks — accounts for 3-week moves.**
- **"As a general rule, when any stock closes higher the 4th consecutive week, it will go higher."**
- **"The 5th week is also very important… The 5th is the day, week, month, or year of Ascension and always marks fast moves up or down."**
### Bull and bear calendar years — the decade pattern (F-6/F-7)
| Year digit | Gann |
|---|---|
| 1 | bear market ends, bull begins (1901, 1911, 1921) |
| 2 | minor bull / a bear-market rally (1902, 1912, 1922, 1932) |
| 3 | starts a bear year; 2nd-year rally may run to Mar/Apr, or a decline bottoms Feb/Mar (1933) |

Calendar years. The 10-year pattern repeats; greatest moves at the ends of 20- and 30-year cycles, strongest at 50- and 60-year ends.
- *Dewey checklist on the decade pattern:* repetition ✔ (Gann cites 3–4 decades each), regularity **not tested**, obvious counter-examples acknowledged by Gann himself (1927). Treat as **non-gating context** (cf. `lib/gann/decadeCycle.ts`).
### Important points to remember (F-7/F-8)
- **"TIME is the most important factor of all… The Time factor will overbalance both Space and Volume."**
- Each stock works out its **1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50 and 60-year cycles from its own base** regardless of others.
- Reversal after extremes: first reversal over **¼ to ½** of the previous move ⇒ trend changed (at least temporarily); when time runs out, space breaks back over **¼, ⅓ or ½** of the last extreme-low-to-high move.
- Five years of higher yearly bottoms then a lower one ⇒ reversal (mirror for tops).
### How to make annual forecasts — Master 20-Year Forecasting Chart (F-8…F-12)
- Previous **10- and 20-year cycles have the most effect**; check **30 years** back; also watch **5, 7, 15, 50-year** repeats. 1935 forecast: 1905, 1915, 1925. **1929 forecast: 1919 (10), 1909 (20), 1899 (30), 1869 (60 — Great Cycle).**
- **Master 20-year Forecasting Chart 1831–1935:** stack 20-year blocks (1831–1850 canal/rail stocks; 1856–96 Gann railroad averages; Dow from 1896): 1861–80, 1881–1900, 1901–20, 1921–40; years numbered 1–20; **"extreme high prices have always been reached" in the 8th and 9th years.**
- **1929:** by the 60-yr cycle 1929 ≈ 1869, 1909, 1919 ⇒ Gann's published 1929 forecast: **top not later than end of August, "Black Friday" in September** (actual top Sep 3). "There is no other way, outside of using the 20 and 60-year cycle that we could have forecast this… so closely."
- 1869–73 vs 1929–33 (Nov 1873 low ↔ Oct 1933 last low).
- **1935:** zone years 1855, 1875, 1895, 1915 and 5th-zone 1865, 1885, 1905, 1925 ⇒ Gann's Oct-1934 forecast: **top Oct 28, secondary top Nov 15–16, 1935** (actual Dow top Nov 20 1935). **Zone-average rule:** Dow crossing 108 in May 1935 = above the average high of all previous 15th-zone years ⇒ bull.
- **1936 outlook:** 16th zone 1856, 1876, 1896, 1916 (60, 40, 20 back) + 6th zone 1866, 1886, 1906, 1926; expects **1896 to repeat** (election year, panicky decline into August), breaks in Jan, Mar, **late May–June (4 yrs from 1932 low, 6 from Apr 1930 high)**, July (nominations), end of August (1896 cycle end), late September; Sep–Nov = **7 years from the Sep–Nov 1929 top/panic**; post-election rally into December.
- Individual stocks: build the same 10- and 20-year cycle charts per stock ("works… even better than on the Averages").
### Fast moves at time angles (F-12…F-15)

### How to trade (F-17…F-21)
- **Before any trade:** check (1) annual forecast (bull/bear year), (2) the stock's own cycle, (3) monthly angles & time periods, (4) weekly — squaring time from top/bottom?, (5) daily — near the square of a recent top/bottom?, (6) price resistance levels / half-way points, (7) **held several days/weeks/months at one level and about to cross/break angles**, (8) volume trend, (9) **the greatest recent reaction** — e.g. prior reactions 5 pts, now 3 off the top in an uptrend near a support angle ⇒ buy, stop 2–3 away; **a reaction exceeding 5 (the prior limit) = trend change → exit**, (10) **"the most important factor… is Geometrical Angles"**; bring up angles from "0", (11) a definite indication, (12) **"Most important of all – always locate the point at which to place a stop loss order."**
- **Monthly + weekly + daily all confirming uptrend ⇒ "a cinch to buy"** (with a stop ≤3 pts); bear year + all three down ⇒ short.
- **Paper-trade first**; "if you make mistakes trading on paper… you are not ready."
- **When to close:** only on a definite indication per the rules, or let the trailed stop be caught.
- **When NOT to trade:** in a narrow 3–5-pt range that hasn't broken bottoms or crossed tops (can last weeks, months, years); **when price is squeezed between two important angles**; after a prolonged decline's narrowing range — wait for angles from the bottom to break or angles from the top to be crossed and an old top crossed.
- **Capital:** divide into **10 equal parts, never risk >10% on one trade; after three consecutive losses reduce the unit** (risk 10% of remaining). $3,000 per 100 shares, ≤3-pt risk ⇒ **"the market would have to beat you 10 consecutive times to wipe out your capital."** Prefer 1–2-pt stops. $300 per 10 shares.
- **Pyramiding:** only in active markets with above-normal volume, after a strong/weak angle position or a range breakout. Add a second lot only after **5 points** in your favour, stop ≤3 pts on both (worst case net −1 pt); add every 5 pts; for stocks **5–75**, after **15–25 pts** in favour start watching for a change. **The big money is the run between accumulation and distribution** — start after double/triple tops are crossed and the zone of accumulation is cleared; buy every 5 pts, stop ≤3 below the last lot (mirror for shorts). Above $100 in fast markets: **7–10 pts apart.** Panic markets: trail ~10 pts, then ~5 pts from the low. **Safest pyramid at extreme levels: 100 → +50 → +30 → +20 (repeat 20) each 5 pts.** **Never add near a double top/bottom**; don't pyramid while in a 10–12-pt range.
- Closing maxim: **"The whole can never exceed all of its parts, and all of the parts make up the whole. If you leave out one of the parts or one of the rules, you do not have a complete trend indicator."**

## Ch. 8 — *Form Reading and Rules for Determining Trend of Stocks* (pp. 237–258; dated **July 6, 1939**; "Statistician and Economist", 82 Wall St / Miami)
### Requirements (p.237): **Knowledge, Patience, Courage, Study.** (Cf. A05's five qualifications.)
### Form reading (p.238): "85% of what we learn is from what we see" — the same formation recurring means the same thing. Prove by past records.
### Capital (p.238): $3,000 per 100 shares, stops ≤3 pts ⇒ 10 trades; **10 equal parts, ≤10% per trade, reduce after 3 consecutive losses**; $15–30 stocks: **$1,500**, first trade with ≤2-pt (ideally 1-pt) risk ⇒ 7–10 trades; **>$100 stocks: $4,000** per 100 shares; $300 per 10 shares.
### Charts (pp. 238–239): busy person: weekly Dow 30 + 15 Utilities + 5–10 leading stocks; monthly on a few low-priced (<10, <20) stocks — buy them when they cross old tops with activity.
### Major and minor trends (p.239)
- Two trends: main and minor (a short reversal of the main). Trading against the trend only on a definite rule signal with close stops. **In a downtrend it is safer to sell rallies than buy reactions; in an uptrend safer to buy reactions than sell rallies.**
### Weekly TREND LINE — green/red (p.239) — codeable
- **Green line:** the first week with a **higher bottom and a higher top** than the previous week, move the line **up to that week's high**; keep moving it to each week's high while higher bottoms and tops continue.
- **Red line:** the first week with a **lower bottom** than the previous week, the line turns red and moves **down to that week's low**; keep moving down while lower bottoms continue. First week with a higher bottom **and** higher top → green again.
- **"BUY WHEN THE RED TREND LINE CHANGES TO GREEN; SELL WHEN THE GREEN TREND LINE CHANGES TO RED."** (Studied on Dow Rails weekly 1896–1914.)
- ⇒ This is the **weekly swing chart** as Gann taught it in 1939 — note the asymmetry: **up-turn requires higher top AND higher bottom; down-turn requires only a lower bottom** (bottom-keyed, like the Overnight Chart). Compare `lib/gann/swingChart.ts` (close-count 3/9-bar) — a different, also-Gann definition.
### Three buying points / three selling points (pp. 240–241)
- **Buy 1 — at old bottoms or old tops** (reaction to them), stop 1–3 pts (high-priced ≤5). Safe the **1st, 2nd or 3rd time; the 4th time is dangerous — "it nearly always goes lower."** Buying 1–3 pts under old levels is OK, but **strongest is holding right at or slightly above** them. Stocks >$100 may react **5 pts under old tops** after crossing them, not more.
- **Buy 2 (safer) — when price crosses former tops / a series of previous weeks' tops** (green line).
- **Buy 3 (safest) — on the secondary reaction after crossing previous weekly tops, once the advance exceeds the greatest rally on the way down**; or when the **first rally from the extreme bottom exceeds *in time* the greatest rally in the preceding bear campaign** (e.g. last bear rally 3–4 weeks → advance >3–4 weeks ⇒ trend up; buy the secondary reaction).
- **Sell 1 — at old tops, or rallies to old bottoms** (1st–3rd time; the 4th "nearly always goes higher"), stop 1–3 above. At >$100 averages can exceed old levels by 5 without changing trend (rare). In weak markets rallies stop **right under old bottoms, ≤2 pts above; 3 above ⇒ strong.**
- **Sell 2 (safer) — break of the previous week's low / a series of weekly bottoms** (red line).
- **Sell 3 (safest) — the secondary rally after breaking previous weekly bottoms / the last reaction's bottom**; or **after the first decline exceeds the greatest reaction in the preceding bull campaign** (points), or **its duration exceeds the last reaction before the top** (e.g. greatest bull reaction 4 weeks — "an average reaction in a bull market" — first decline >4 weeks ⇒ change).
### Formations (pp. 241–245) — breakaway points
- After accumulation/distribution comes a **BREAKAWAY POINT**; money is made fastest there. "Similar action of the market occurs around the same month years apart."
- **Bottoms:** **"V" sharp** (no retest); **"U" flat** — 3–10+ weeks in a narrow range, crossing the intermediate tops = breakaway (Rails Mar–Apr 1898, 56–60, four tests); **"W" double** — buy when it crosses the middle of the W; **"WV" triple** — buy on crossing the second W top (Dec 1899 72½ / Jun 1900 73 / Sep 1900 73¼ → +44 pts in 28 weeks); **"WW" four-bottom** — buy on crossing the middle point of the second W (1903–04 → +27 without returning to 99).
- **Tops:** **"A" sharp** — after 17–26+ weeks of advance with only 10-day–2-week reactions, sharp decline; sell the secondary rally, safer on breaking the last leg of the A / the first-decline bottom; **flat top** — narrow multi-week range, sell on breaking the series of weekly bottoms (Rails May–Jul 1911, 2½-pt range, 8 weeks); **"M" double** — reaction of **3–7 weeks** between tops; sell on breaking the middle of the M; **"MA" triple** — 2nd/3rd tops slightly lower after a long advance = major decline signal; **"the more time between tops, the stronger"**; sell on breaking the A's bottom = breakaway (1906 → 1907 panic −57 in 11 months); **"MM" four-top** — sell on breaking the last reaction low.
### Sections (pp. 244–245)
- Bull: **(1) advance off the final bottom + secondary reaction; (2) advance above prior weeks' highs + reaction; (3) new high — often the end; (4) often a fourth run — "the most important to watch for the end."** **Minor campaigns (≤1 year) often run out in two sections**, especially from a sharp bottom/top. Bear mirror: (1) sharp severe decline + secondary rally (shorts safer); (2) lower + moderate rally; (3) still lower; (4) watch for bottom. **Extreme cases (1929–32) up to 7 sections.**
### Change in the main trend — SPACE and TIME rules (pp. 245–246) — codeable
- **Space:** "When a decline in points **exceeds the greatest decline of a previous reaction by one or more points**, it is an indication of a change in trend." Apply after **3+ sections**: first break back more than the campaign's greatest reaction ⇒ main trend has changed or will soon (a secondary rally usually still follows — sell rallies).
- **Time:** with 3–4 sections run, **when a reaction's duration exceeds the greatest time of any previous reaction**, the main trend has changed (greatest 4 weeks ⇒ **5 consecutive weeks down** = change; shorts on the secondary rally). Mirror in bear markets.
- A reversal after only the 2nd section matters less than after the 3rd/4th.
### Ranges, accumulation/distribution (pp. 246–248)
- **2–6 or 10–13 weeks in a narrow range, then crossing tops / breaking bottoms of previous weeks ⇒ go with it; the longer in range, the bigger the move** (Rails 1897: 2-pt range Jan 16–Mar 20 broke down; Apr 10–May 22 2-pt range crossed 51 → bull).
- **Distribution on the side** (after 3rd/4th section, sharp decline, rally, then long range several points below the high): short at the top of the range, **safer on breaking its low (breakaway)**. **Accumulation on the side** at bear ends: buy at the range bottom, **safer when its tops are crossed.**
- **Last stage:** in bull finales reactions get **smaller** until a sharp reversal; in bear finales rallies get smaller ⇒ never buck the trend in the last stage.
- **Range of bottoms/tops:** **within 1–3 points = double/triple** bottom/top; strong markets break a bottom by only 1 (extreme 2) then rally; **broken by 3 full points ⇒ lower before any important rally**; **3 points above old tops ⇒ nearly always higher**; false moves occur at the end of bull/bear markets.
- **How far below old tops after crossing them:** strong = stop at the old top or 1–2 below, **seldom >3**; **>5 below ⇒ trend reversed (for the time being)** — depends on section; a signal in the last section is most important. Mirror in bear: rallies to old bottoms stop ≤1–2 (avg ≤3) above; **>5 above ⇒ going higher.**
- **10–12-pt swings in a range after 3–4 sections = accumulation/distribution**; break of range bottom ⇒ lower, cross of tops ⇒ higher. **An advancing market with 10–12-pt reactions, then a 20–24-pt reaction; thereafter a decline of >20 from any top usually runs 30–40.**
### Strongest/weakest (p.248)
- Strongest: after a prolonged (esp. sharp) decline with small rallies, **2nd or 3rd higher bottom, then crossing a previous rally top**; **"an advance usually starts from the 3rd or 4th higher bottom"** — the long advance with small reactions. Weakest: **3rd or 4th lower top**, then break of the last low.
### Minor trend (pp. 248–249)
- Top at the same level ≥2 weeks (esp. narrow) then break under the bottom of 2+ weeks ⇒ minor trend down; bottom ≥2 weeks in a narrow range then cross the tops of 2–3 weeks ⇒ minor trend up. **Dull, narrow market ⇒ a change is coming — follow the breakout.**
### Duration of minor moves (p.249)
- **Bull-market minor reactions 3–4 weeks; "as a rule in the 4th week they will rally and close higher"**; sometimes only 2 weeks; after a 3–4-week decline, a minor rally, then a break under the 3rd/4th-week bottom ⇒ bigger change, probably the main trend. Extreme: after a secondary rally a bull decline can run **6–7 weeks**, seldom more.
- **Bear-market rallies 2–4 weeks; if a rally holds into the 5th week, it is likely to run into the 6th or 7th ⇒ watch for an important change.** After a 3–4-week bear rally and a secondary reaction, **crossing the high of that rally ⇒ trend changing, at least temporarily.**
### First change in trend by opening and closing prices (pp. 249–250) — codeable
- Use the **weekly chart with open and close**. **The closing price is most important.**
- **Top signal:** at an old top / late section in an active market, if price **does not trade more than 1 point above Monday's open** and then **closes Saturday at or near the week's low** ⇒ first weekly indication of a change (confirm with time/space/weekly-bottom rules).
- **Bottom signal:** after a sharp fast decline, if price falls into mid/late week, then reverses and **closes Saturday at/near or above Monday's open** ⇒ especially strong sign of reversal (confirm).
- **Weekly closes under old bottoms / previous weeks' bottoms (or above old tops) = weakness (strength).**
### New high / new low ground (p.250) — projection bands
- A move into all-time new highs ⇒ typically **7, 10, 15, 20–24 points** (esp. in the 1st–2nd section; in the 3rd–4th section less). **Watch resistance at +7, +10, ~+15, +20–24 into new territory.** Works for stocks priced **50–125**; wider above 125, 200, 300. Mirror for new lows.
### Daily chart (p.251): in very active/final-stage markets keep daily charts with the same trend-line rules — first minor change, later confirmed weekly. **"Don't try to get ahead of the market… wait until it shows a definite change in trend."**
### Appendices (pp. 252–258)
- **Dow Rails formations 1896–1914** (dated list of V, flat, M, W, triple tops/bottoms with week counts and ranges, e.g. "Jul 29–Sep 9 1899 flat top, 7 weeks in 2-pt range, 1st lower top"; "Sep 29 1900 triple bottom, 3rd higher bottom — strong buying point"; "May 23–Jul 11 1914 flat top 9 weeks in 2-pt range — panicky decline followed").

## Ch. 9 — *Resistance Levels* (pp. 259–277; dated **July 6, 1939**)
"Everything in existence is based on exact proportion and perfect relation. There is no chance in nature." (Faraday: "nothing in the Universe but mathematical points of force.") **"Every stock makes a top or bottom on some exact mathematical point in proportion to some previous high or low level."**
### How to compute (R-1…R-3)
1. **Range of fluctuation ÷ 8** → ⅛ points (buy/sell points when price stops there **and the weekly trend line turns**; may hold 3–7 days or several weeks).
2. **Range ÷ 3** → ⅓, ⅔ points — very strong, esp. when near other levels or on very wide moves.
3. **Highest selling price ÷ 8 and ÷ 3** (also each lower top). After breaking the ½ of the range, a stock often falls to the **½ of the highest price**; advancing, it often crosses the ½ of the highest price then meets resistance at the ½ of the range.
- **Priority of moves:** (1) life extreme low → extreme high; (2) divisions of the all-time high price; (3) each campaign of ≥1 year (÷8); (4) **"take a third or fourth lower top and divide it by 9"**.
- **Secondary bottoms/tops:** after a bear low, first rally, secondary higher bottom → **½ between the first-rally high and the secondary low = strong support** (Dow 1932–33: 81½ & 49½ → 61 ... wait printed "half-way point 61"? actually (81½+40½)/2 = 61 — Gann pairs the final low and first rally here). After a bull top: first decline low + secondary lower top → **their ½ is strong resistance** (1937: 163 & 190½ → 179¼ with the Mar 1937 high 195½ context). Also ½ between secondary bottom (Feb 1933 49½) and secondary top (Aug 1937 190½) = **120 → Apr 11 1939 low 120.**
### Order of resistance levels (R-3)
- Advancing past **¼** → next watch **½** (gravity center) → **⅝** → **¾ ("the next and strongest after ½ is crossed")** → on very wide ranges **⅞ "will often mark the top of an advance."** Always with weekly trend lines/formations.
### The half-way point (R-3/R-4) — "you can make a fortune by following this one rule alone"
- ½ = (extreme low + extreme high)/2 — "the balancing point." **Buy/sell at it with a 1, 2 or 3-pt stop** (by price level). **"The wider the range and the longer the time period, the more important."** Major = life/major-move halves on 25–100+-pt ranges; minor = between minor top and bottom; **"reactions usually run back half of the last move."**
- **½ ↔ ⅝ / ⅜ rule:** if the ½-to-⅝ distance is ≥5–10 pts and ½ is crossed ⇒ runs to **⅝**, meets resistance, often reacts back to ½ (buy again). Mirror: breaking ½ ⇒ **⅜**, then rally to ½. In narrow ranges a stock oscillates **⅜ ↔ ⅝ around ½** ("moving in ¼ of the full range").
- After several reactions from ½ then a break through ⇒ next level on the "Resistance Level Card" or the next old top (mirror for support).
- **Strength: holding ≥1 pt above the ½; weakness: failing to reach the ½ by ≥1 pt** then breaking the trend line.
- After the main (life) ½ breaks → next the ½ of a previous move; also **the ½ of the highest selling price** is a stronger support than minor halves "until crossed by 1, 2 or 3 points" (price-dependent).
### Resistance points near the same levels (R-4/R-5) — Gann's clustering rule
- **When two half-way points (or any two levels) fall near the same level, add them and divide by 2** — the midpoint is often the support/sell point.
- Look for **3 or 4 levels around one price — "the more you find, the stronger"**; take the **highest and lowest of the cluster and average them** for the resistance point. *(Primary source for `lib/strat/levels.ts#combineNearbyLevels` — AGENTS.md cites the rule; this is the lesson text.)*
- In fast, big-volume moves don't assume a stop at a level unless price **holds 1–2 days** there; consider the section (3rd/4th).
### Average & extreme fluctuations (R-5)
- **Dow normal swings 10–12 pts; exceeding that ⇒ 20–24; then 30–40.** For huge ranges (28½ in 1896 → 386 in 1929; 386 → 40½) **divide by 16 and 32** as well as 8.
- Time is needed at bottoms (accumulation) and tops (distribution); longer campaigns need more.
- **When stocks fall back under old tops of previous campaigns**, use the levels between the old bottoms and those tops (Dow after 1929 below 119⅝ (1919 top) → use levels from 28½, 38½, 42, 52 to 119⅝; above 120 → higher tops, key 195½–197).
### Small gains in the last stage (R-5/R-6)
- Successive runs above prior tops getting **smaller** (+20/−10, +15/−5–7½, +10/−5) ⇒ top near (except very active high-volume finales). Mirror: smaller declines ⇒ selling pressure waning — but panics end with a large final wave and tiny rallies.
- **In the last stages use only the half-way point of the short/minor moves — especially the final move; "when it is exceeded by 3 full points, the trend usually reverses."**
### LOST MOTION (R-6) — primary definition
- "As there is lost motion in every kind of machinery, so there is lost motion in the stock market due to momentum… **The average lost motion is 1⅞ points.**" Very active, heavy-volume moves **often go 1⅞ pts beyond a half-way point or other strong level but not 3 full points**; same on declines. Analogy: a ball dropped through a hole bored through the earth overshoots the gravity center and finally settles on it.
- ⇒ **This is the primary text for `lib/gann/entryTrigger.ts`'s lost-motion allowance** (AGENTS.md cites A8 *Commodities*; the 1939 course states it with the number **1⅞** and the **"not 3 full points"** ceiling). GSPS translation must scale by price/volatility (1⅞ pts on a ~$100 Dow of 1939); the *structure* is: tolerance band ≈ 1⅞ units with a hard invalidation at 3 units.

## Ch. 11A — *Review of Bull and Bear Markets… Buying and Selling Points 1903–1939* (pp. 308–348; signed **August 4, 1939**)
A week-by-week walk through every Dow Industrials campaign from Nov 1903 to Aug 1939, labelling each pivot BUYING LEVEL / SELLING LEVEL and giving the reason. No new techniques, but it is **the most complete worked application of the weekly-chart rules in the whole course**. It fixes their exact wording and the order in which Gann applied them. The rules as he actually used them:

### Trend-change rules (the "Space Rule" / previous-reaction limit), applied every campaign
- **End-of-campaign review, every time:** record (a) the **greatest** counter-trend move of the campaign, (b) the **last** counter-trend move, and (c) the last cluster of 3–4 weekly bottoms/tops. Then:
  - retracing more than the **last** reaction = **first signal**
  - breaking the cluster of weekly bottoms/tops = **second, definite signal**
  - retracing more than the **greatest** reaction = **main trend has changed** (third signal)
  Worked instance: Sep 1929. Last small reaction 10 pts, weekly bottoms at 336½, greatest reaction 44 pts. Breaking back over 10 was the first signal, under 336½ the second, over 44 the third. ⇒ **a three-stage, escalating confirmation, not one threshold.** "Exceed by 1 point": to confirm a bull market, the advance must be the greatest bear-market rally **+1 point** (1903: greatest rally 8 ⇒ 9 pts confirms).
- **Minor trend** turns when price **crosses the tops of the previous 2–3 weeks** (up) or **breaks the lows of the previous 2–3 weeks** (down). Gann uses 2, 3, 4, even 5–9 weeks' tops/bottoms; **3 weeks is the default**. "Safer" entries are always *after* this weekly crossing rather than at the level itself. Recurring pattern: an aggressive buy at support with a stop, and a **safer** buy after the weekly top is crossed.
- **Bull-market reaction depth at extreme price levels:** "reactions or advances can run to around **40 points** when prices are at an extreme level" (1929). At high prices, breaking **2** points under prior lows "was not enough… it should break at least **3 points** under the previous lows to show a definite change." ⇒ the 3-point allowance again (cf. Ch. 9's lost motion: never 3 full points).
- **A reaction under an old top should never exceed ~5 points if the main trend is to continue up** (Mar 1925: 4⅝ under the old top was acceptable).
- **Advances into new high ground run 7, 10, 15, 20 or 24 points, then react.** "The safest rule says, wait until the minor trend turns by breaking under the previous weekly bottom." Worked: 1916 ran 7⅛ over the 1906 top, 1919 ran 9½ over the 1916 top.

### Formation and position rules, with worked examples
- **Double / triple / fourth top or bottom:** a third top at the same level = "SAFE SELLING LEVEL". A **fourth** time at a level usually goes through (1912, 1924 at 105: "the FOURTH TIME AT THIS LEVEL … a definite indication of a BULL MARKET"). A **fourth or fifth lower top** is "a sure and SAFE INDICATION TO SELL" (1938).
- **Third higher bottom = safe buy**; a series of higher bottoms (1939: first through fifth) marks accumulation.
- **Old top becomes bottom** (and old bottom becomes top), invoked on nearly every pivot. Support and resistance are always the prior weekly clusters, looked up across decades (e.g. 1932's 40½ low supported at the 1897–1903 lows of 38½–42).
- **Dull, narrow weeks** (range ≤1–1½ pts) at a top after a run = selling; at a bottom after a decline = selling exhausted. Very small volume at a bottom = liquidation has run its course.
- **"SIGNAL TOP" (defined, Sep 7 1929):** "making a new high and the same week breaking the low of the previous week and closing lower." A weekly key-reversal (outside-down week closing lower) = final top. Mirror (May 1928): opened at the top, closed under the prior week's low = first signal of a top. Mirror strength: "closing at the top… the highest closing in history… indication of higher prices" (Aug 1927).
- **Round numbers:** "Watch selling at all even levels, that is, 100, 200, 300" (1927); "**100 is always the SELLING POINT**" (1933). Once 200 was crossed (1928) prices "stay above that level until the Bull Campaign is completed."
- **Secondary reaction / secondary rally:** "after the first sharp decline… running 3 weeks or more, there is **always** a secondary rally" (safe to sell on). After the first sharp advance from a panic bottom there is always a secondary reaction (safe to buy on). Dec 1929: the secondary reaction retraced **more than 50%** of the first run-up — "the PLACE TO BUY with close stop."
- **Last stage of a bear market:** "the lower prices get… the smaller the rallies" until the final clean-out. **Panic days:** "cover shorts and buy on panic days" / "always buy in panics for a quick rally."
- **Accumulation / distribution "on the side":** e.g. 8 months in an 8-pt range, 1903–04; Dec 1910 – Jul 1911, 6–7 pts. **Jul 1933 – Mar 1935 held 91 weeks** within 2 pts of the Jul 17/21 1933 extremes (27-pt range). "This long period of time indicated that when the averages crossed 112… they would go very much higher" (to 195½). ⇒ **the length of a sideways period scales the move that follows.**
- **Reaction timing in bull markets:** a reaction exceeding 3–4 weeks typically runs **~6–7 weeks** ("the average reaction in a bull market when the decline exceeds 4 weeks"). 3-week and 6-week reactions recur as "natural".
- **Psychology (Gann's own maxim):** "A Bull Market begins in gloom and ends in glory" (Mar 1933 bank holiday vs 1929). The public "buys on hope and sells on fear."
- **Fast markets:** "by keeping up a **daily** high and low chart and watching the first time tops of previous days are crossed, you get the first indication to buy." ⇒ drop to the daily chart when weekly swings are too fast (the correspondence of timeframes).

## Ch. 12 — *Volume of Sales* (pp. 355–369; signed **August 12, 1939**)
Volume is the **fourth factor**, after Formations, Time and Resistance Levels. It is "the real driving power behind the market", showing whether supply or demand is increasing. It is always used **in combination with** the other three.
### The four rules for culminations by volume (verbatim substance)
1. After a prolonged bull campaign or rapid advance, a **large increase in volume marks the end**, at least temporarily. After a sharp decline on heavy volume, a **secondary rally on decreasing volume** ⇒ final top is in and the main trend will turn down.
2. After a **second lower top**, if the stock gets dull and narrow sideways and then **breaks out on increased volume** ⇒ further decline.
3. After a prolonged decline (weeks, months or years), **volume decreases and the range narrows** at bottom. This is "one of the sure signs that liquidation is running its course."
4. After the first sharp advance out of a bear market, the **secondary reaction on decreasing volume**, followed by an advance on heavier volume ⇒ higher levels.
- These apply to total NYSE volume (daily, weekly or monthly) **and** to individual stocks.
- **Summary:** "SALES INCREASE NEAR THE TOP AND DECREASE NEAR THE BOTTOM." The exception is abnormal, fast panics (Oct–Nov 1929), which culminate on large volume with a sharp bottom and swift rebound.
### Additional volume findings
- **"Largest volume of sales before final top":** the peak volume often comes **before** the actual high. At the high itself, volume is smaller than in prior months or weeks (the public has already loaded up). Worked: Jan 1936 largest volume, then Apr 1936 high; Jan 1937 largest volume, then Mar 8 1937 final high.
- **Shares per point (volume efficiency), Gann's own metric:**
  - Jul 1934 → Oct 1936: 88,860 shares per Dow point.
  - Oct 1936 → Mar 1937 top: **151,197 shares per point**, nearly double. "This **increased volume on a smaller gain** was an indication that the market was nearing top."
  ⇒ Codeable: rising volume-per-unit-of-price-progress at the end of an advance = effort without result = top.
- **Float turnover:** Chrysler 1928, the last 15-week "grand rush" (63⅝ → 140½, never breaking a prior week's low) turned the whole capital stock over **twice**, and **half of it in the last 2 weeks**. The record weekly volume came in the top week. In Jan–Apr 1934, **the entire capital stock traded within a 10-point range** after a 52-point advance ⇒ distribution ⇒ change in trend.
- **Sideways distribution:** the minor trend turns down, the rally can't reach old highs, and the stock moves sideways while "people buy the stock on the reaction because they think it is cheap."
- **Secondary rally/reaction volume test:** the Aug 1937 secondary rally came to within 5 pts of the top on **≤ ⅓ of the volume** of the first top ⇒ "a signal to sell the market short". The mirror, May 1938: a secondary reaction on very small volume ⇒ buy.
- **Smallest monthly volume in years at a low = "a sure sign of bottom"** (Feb 1933; Sep 1934 at 12M shares).
- Time cross-references inside the volume chapter:
  - "watch for a change in trend **one year, two years, or three years from any important top and bottom**" (Jul 1933 top ⇒ Jul 1934 bottom)
  - "always watch for a change in trend **at the end of a year**" (Chrysler +52⅝ in 51 weeks)
  - "At no time from April 1930 to July 1932 had the averages… rallied over two months. Therefore… they would have to advance **three full months or more**" (the third-month rule, restated)
- **Space equality:** the Nov 1935 advance equalled the 1933 campaign's points (61 vs 60) ⇒ "time to watch for at least a temporary change in trend." Price-move **equality with a prior campaign** is a watch signal. The 1934 Chrysler low of 29¼ was "practically **one-half** of 60⅜."
- Gann attributes lower post-1934 volume per point to SEC regulation, and closes with an anti-regulation editorial (historical context only; nothing to implement).

## Ch. 13 — *Mathematical Formula for Market Predictions: The Master Mathematical Price, Time and Trend Calculator* (Square of 144) (pp. 370–379; signed **September 29, 1953**, among Gann's last writings)
Instructions for a transparent overlay: a 144×144 grid ruled in 9-unit sections, with red 45° angles on the squares of 9, green 2×1 and 1×2 angles, green straight lines at thirds, and an "Inner Square" drawn from 72.
### Why 144
- "The square of 12 is always important… 12 months in a year." **144 is "the GREAT SQUARE"** and "works better than any other square both for TIME AND PRICE because it contains all of the squares from 1 to 144."
- The grid is divided into 9s "because 9 is the highest digit." 9 spaces = 9 days/weeks/months = 9¢ grain = 9 pts stocks = 90 pts cotton.
### The Great Cycle of the Square of 144
- 144² = **20,736** units. Gann's calendar conversions (days → weeks → months → years) show the unit is **days**: 2,962 weeks = 681 months 23 days = **56 years 9 months 23 days** (the text's "20,736 weeks or months" is loose wording).
- Fractions: ½ = 10,368 d (28 yr 5 mo 8 d; the monthly section's "28 yr 9 mo 23 d" is a transcription inconsistency); ¼ = 5,184 d (14 yr 2 mo 19 d); ⅛ = 2,592 d (7 yr 1 mo 10 d); 1/16 = 1,296 d (42 mo 20 d); 1/32 = 648 d (21 mo 10 d, "close to 22½ months, which is 1/16 of the circle"); 1/64 = 324; 1/128 = 162; 1/256 = **81 = 9²**.
- Weekly: 1,481 wk (½), 740 (¼), 370 (⅛), 185 (1/16), 92 wk 4 d (1/32), ~46 (1/64).
- "**The 14-year cycle is always very important because it is 2 seven-year periods.** 14 years = 168 months and **169 months is the square of 13**."
### The Master Numbers: 3, 5, 7, 9, 12 (Gann's own ranking and reasons)
- **9** is most important ("9 digits added together equal 45"). **7** is next (the Bible; 7 days in the week). **7² = 49**, with multiples 98, 147 and **196 (= 14²)**. **5** is "the balancing number between 1 and 9". 5² = 25; 50 is "just 1 over the square of 7, making **49 to 50 very important for a change in trend**"; 75; **100 = 10²**. **3**: 3×3 = 9, "must be used in every way possible" (21, 15, 27, 36 = 6²). **12**: disciples, months, zodiac signs; the 12s in 144 are 12, 24 … 144.
- **63–64** (7×9 and 8²), **84** (7×12, "of very great importance"), **90** (10×9, ¼ circle) and **108** (9×12, ¾ of 144).
- Gann's reasons are partly scriptural and numerological (Tier A primary, but **B2/numerology caution applies**: this is Gann's stated rationale, not a market proof). The **arithmetic** content (the fractions of 144 and 360, the squares) is what is operational.
### 144 ↔ 360 correspondence
- 360 = 2½ × 144; 180 = 1¼ × 144; 90 = ⅝ × 144.
- Fractions of 144: 9 = 1/16, 18 = ⅛, 27 = 3/16 (text says 3/8), 36 = ¼, 45 = 5/16, 48 = ⅓, 54 = 3/8, 63 = 7/16, 72 = ½, 81 = 9/16, 90 = ⅝, 99 = 11/16, 108 = ¾, 117 = 13/16, 126 = ⅞, 135 = 15/16.
- **"Strongest points" of the square: ¼, ⅓, ⅔, ⅜, ½, ⅝, ¾, ⅞ and the full square.** "Points where the most angles cross are strongest for resistance in PRICE and TIME."
- **Triangle points** (green crossings): 36, 48, 72, 96, 108, 144. **Squares within the square:** 36, 45, 54, 63, 72, 90, 108, 144.
- **Time = price ⇒ "TIME and PRICE is SQUARE… watch for change in trend"** (price 36 at time 36; at 72/72 they sit on the 45° angle at the half-way point).
### Five factors and the close — directly codeable
- Three points on any bar-chart period: **low, high and the ½ (range midpoint)**.
- "**FIVE FACTORS FOR TIME AND PRICE:** high, low, halfway point, opening and closing prices. **The trend is indicated by the closing price… If price closes above the half-way point or near the high, the trend is up. If it closes below the half-way point or near the low… the trend is down, at least temporarily.**" ⇒ a per-bar trend reading, Gann's own: close vs (H+L)/2.
- Price has three aspects: price, time & volume, and **pitch** (the angle). **Four factors: PRICE, TIME, VOLUME, VELOCITY.** "**Time is the most important factor because when time is up volume increases and the velocity… increases.**"
### Where to watch for changes in trend
- Time at **½ of 144**, at the end of the square, and at ⅓, ⅔, ¼, ¾.
- Watch **the square in time of**:
  - the highest price
  - the minor highs and lows
  - the lowest price
  - the second/third higher bottom
  - the range
- **Worked example (wheat):** lowest 28¢ ⇒ every 28 months squares it; May-option high 325 ⇒ 325 months; low 44 ⇒ 44 months; range 281 ⇒ 281 months/weeks/days. 2×144 = 288 ⇒ watch **281–288**. 6½ × 44 = 286 ⇒ also inside that window. ⇒ **Convergence of several independent squarings into one narrow window is the signal.**
- **Placement rules:** lay 0 at the extreme low, the extreme high, 0 itself, the ½ of the range, or ½ of the highest price. Put the **centre (72) on the halfway point in price**, "the gravity center". "**Never overlook** the extreme high and low price. Also most important ½ of high to low and ½ of the highest price any commodity or stock ever sold."
- "**When prices pass out of one square into another, a change of trend usually takes place.**" Start a new square every 144 time units. Count **leap years** (add a day) when computing exact day and week counts.
- **Hourly charts:** "When markets are very active… keep an **hourly** High and Low Chart… the Hourly Chart will give the first change in trend." 144 hours at 5 h/day ≈ 28 days 4 h. ⇒ a Gann-sourced justification for intraday charting in fast markets.
- **Chart hierarchy (restated):** daily = first indication; weekly = next in importance; **monthly = greatest importance for changes in the main trend.**
### Circle of 360° divisions (price and time)
- Order of importance:
  1. ÷2 (180)
  2. ÷3 (120, 240)
  3. ÷4 (90, 270)
  4. ÷8 (45s)
  5. ÷16 (22½)
  6. ÷32 (11¼)
  7. ÷64 (5⅝)

  Also ÷6 (60, 300), **÷12 (30, 150, 210, 330: "works out accurately for time periods")** and ÷24 (15° ≈ 15 days; 15, 75, 105, 165, 195, 285, 345).
- Full **table of 64ths of the circle** (5⅝ … 360) is reproduced in the source. Rows 8 and 16 (45s and 90s) are marked most important.
- **Squares 1…19² (1, 4, 9 … 361)** are "important degrees in the circle" for time and price.
- **Method:** "always figure how many points… prices are up from the extreme low or minor lows and… down from the extreme highs or minor highs… also… above or below the main halfway point or the minor halfway points or **gravity centers**. The important halfway points form very close to these natural degrees in the circle."
- Worked example (May soybeans 1948): high 436¾, low 67. Midpoint 251⅞ ≈ 45/64 (253⅛); ½ of the high, 218⅜ ≈ 39/64 (219⅜). Cash low 44 ≈ 45. The 67 low ≈ 67½ (halfway 45–90), with triple bottoms at 67–69. Time: Dec 28 1932 low → **Dec 28 1947 = 15 yr = 180 months (½ circle)**; high on **Jan 15 1948, 18 days after**.

## Ch. 14 — *Master Calculator for Weekly Time Periods* (Square of 52) (pp. 380–386; signed **January 10, 1955**, Gann's last dated lesson)
### Construction
- 52 weeks × 7 days = **364 days**, so each year the weekly count gains one day (7 days in 7 years), plus a day per leap year. For long spans, compute the calendar days and divide by 7. "You start time periods **from the actual dates of important highs and lows, not from the first day of each month or year.**"
- **52² = 2,704**: as days, 386 wk 2 d ≈ 7 yr 5 mo, "very close to the important cycle of **7½ years (90 months)**". As hours, 2,704/24 = 112⅔ days.
- The calculator spans 104 weeks (2 yr) and extends to 208, 312, 416 and **520 (10-year cycle)**. The price scale matches: 104/208/312/416/520 units ($1 per share for stocks).
### Divisions of the year (weekly) — Gann's weights
- ⅛ = 6½ wk; ¼ = 13; ⅓ = 17; ⅜ = 19½; **½ = 26 ("a most important time and resistance level")**; ⅝ = 32½; ⅔ = 35; **¾ = 39 ("very important for a change in trend")**; ⅞ = 45½; 1 = 52.
- **Ranking, "MOST IMPORTANT TIME PERIODS":**
  1. **Anniversaries (1, 2, 3+ years) of important highs and lows**
  2. ½ year
  3. ¾ year (39 weeks)
  4. ⅓ (17 weeks) and ⅔ (35 weeks)
### Day-level time counts (from any important high or low)
- **3½ days** (½ of 7): "Always watch the **3rd and 4th day** from any important high or low for a minor change in trend, which later may become a major change."
- **7-day periods: 14 days is the most important, 21 next.** "Reactions will often run 2 weeks, and sometimes 3 weeks and then resume the main trend. Rallies in a Bear Market often run 14 days, sometimes 21." (Consistent with Ch. 11B: 2–5 weeks typical.)
- **49 days (7²)**: start watching after day 42, but the first sign may not come until **day 45–46 (⅛ year)**. **23 days (1/16 year)** is also important. Then **63–65** (7×9, 8²), **81** (9²), **90–91** (¼ year = 7×13, "very great importance") and ~**182** (½ year).
### Yearly cycles — the 1955 list
- "Important yearly cycles are **1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 18, 20, 21, 22½, 24, 25, 27, 28, 30, 40, 45, 49, 56, 60, 84, and 90, which is the Great Cycle**."
- Divide each cycle by ½ (most important), ⅛, ⅓ and ⅔. For example, 90 yr = 1,080 mo: ½ = 45 yr, ¼ = 22½, ⅛ = 11¼, 1/16 = 5⅝.
- ⚠ **"Great Cycle" / "Master Time Period" is not one fixed number across Gann's own writing:**
  - 1931 (*Wall Street Stock Selector*, per the catalog): the 20-year cycle is the most important
  - 1935 (Ch. 4 of this course): **60 years = "GREAT CYCLE – MASTER TIME PERIOD"**
  - 1953 (Ch. 13): the Square-of-144 great cycle = 20,736 days ≈ **56¾ years**
  - 1955 (this chapter): **90 years = "the Great Cycle"**

- **Multiples of 7 years**: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, **49 (most important, 7²)**, 56, 63 (7×9), 81 (9²). **Prices in sevens too**: 98 = 2×49, 126 = 2×63, 162 = 2×81.
- Also watch the 3, 5, 7, 10, 15 (½ of 30), 20 (⅓ of 60) and **30-year (360 months, "a complete cycle")** cycles on weekly work.
- **Confluence rule:** "When **⅓ of a year from one important low comes out at the same time that ¼ or ½ of a year from another important top or bottom**, it is of great importance." ⇒ Gann's own statement that **coincidence of independent time counts** is the signal (cf. the Ch. 13 wheat 281–288 window).
### Price, time and the "fourth dimension"
- Price, in order: (1) lowest, (2) highest, (3) **the ½ point, "mean or average… gravity center"**, with 45° angles drawn from it, "the most important for price resistance", (4) volume, "the power which drives… but TIME is the most essential element and when Time is completed, the volume of sales starts to move the market."
- Time sections: daily, weekly, monthly and yearly highs and lows. **"The Weekly and the Yearly Time Periods are most important for trend indications and for changes in trend."**
- Day quarters: sunrise, noon, sunset and midnight. Noon (90°) and midnight (180° from noon) are most important. This is astronomical framing with no operational rule.
- **Pitch or trend (the "4th dimension")** is read mostly off the **45° angle**. Above 45° is acute and fast; below it is flat and creeping, and a market can "regain important angles and increase the pitch."
- **"When a Time Cycle is completed, Volume increases and the market begins to move up faster or down faster."**
- **Three ways to square time and price:**
  1. balance the lowest price and the highest price with time in weeks
  2. balance the range
  3. balance price and time in weekly periods on the Square of 52
- Placement: at 0 below the price, at the low, "Top" at the high on its exact date, and **26 (the ½) over ½ of the highest price or ½ of the range**. The Inner Square of 45° angles runs from 26, and all 45° lines cross at ¼, ½ and ¾, balancing time and price.

- **Midseason dates: May 5, Aug 5, Nov 8, Feb 4.** "Important changes in trend occur around these midseason dates." ⇒ compare with the fixed annual calendar in `lib/gann/timeCycles.ts`.
