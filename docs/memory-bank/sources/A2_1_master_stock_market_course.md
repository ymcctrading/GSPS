## Ch. 1 — *Speculation a Profitable Profession: A Course of Instructions on Stocks* (pp. 1–44; dated **May 18, 1954**, Gann "nearing my 76th birthday", "52 years' experience")
### Foreword / preparation (pp. 1–3)
- Prepare by looking up **the greatest advance, the greatest decline, and the greatest time period from high or low** a stock has ever had.
- Most profits come in **active, fast-moving** markets (1924–29; Sep 29–Nov 15 1929; Nov 13 1929–Apr 17 1930; Jun 1949–Jun 1950; Jun 13–Jul 14 1950 fast decline; Sep 1953–Apr 1954 fast advance).
- **Keep out of slow trading markets** until a definite change in trend (Nov 1946–Nov 1949 = consolidation/accumulation before a bull market).
- **Cross-currents:** some stocks decline while others advance (1952–54 Studebaker/Chrysler/Celanese down; Vanadium/steels/DuPont/aircraft up).
- Select the leading **group** from the group's average/total value breaking into new highs, then the strongest stock in it (aircraft 1953–54).
- **Low-priced stocks disappoint** — tie up capital; can sit in narrow ranges 5–10+ years then fall. Best: **higher-priced stocks bought after they break into new high ground** with activity.
- New industries / "stocks of the future" (1954): uranium/atomic-energy (Babcock & Wilcox, Bendix, Dow, DuPont, General Dynamics, GE, Newport News, Phillips, Sylvania, Union Carbide, Vanadium, Westinghouse; Vanadium/Molybdenum/General Dynamics best; New Park Mining, Fairchild speculative) — but **buy only when total NYSE value is in uptrend and the group is in uptrend.**
- Outlook May 1954: total NYSE value at record; trend up since 1932, last extreme low Apr 1942, current swing from Jun 1949 ⇒ **expect a decline in the last half of 1954; be cautious buying.**
### Fundamental rules (pp. 3–6)
- **Uptrend = higher bottoms and higher tops; downtrend = lower tops and lower bottoms.** Prices can range for weeks/months/years; after a long time, a break into **new lows ⇒ lower**, an advance **above old tops ⇒ higher**. Hence keep charts far back to know where the stock is between extreme high and extreme low.
- **Charts to keep:** weekly (2–3 yrs back), monthly (≥10 yrs), yearly (5, 10 or 20 yrs); daily (a few months) once a stock becomes very active. ("One good picture is worth 10,000 words.")
- **Follow the main trend:** never too high to buy / too low to sell while trend holds; never short *because* it is high; never take profits *because* price is high; never buy *because* it is low.
- Always a **STOP LOSS**; several small losses are recovered by one large profit; large losses are hard to recover.
- **"Prove all things and hold fast to that which is good"** — buying new highs / selling new lows is most profitable; prove it to yourself.
- **Prolonged advances ending in a runaway market come down faster than they went up** — keep dailies at the end of fast moves and weeklies for the first change in trend.
- **After a rapid advance, the first sharp decline lasts one month to as much as seven weeks** and corrects the overbought position ⇒ secondary advance follows.
- Catching the extremes at the end of **any great time cycle** can yield large money in one year in fast markets.
- **No fixed ideas / fixed prices** for how high/low; don't buy/sell at a price you fix (hope/fear).
- **Protect profits with stops** — profits are capital. "When you are out of the market, the only thing you can lose or miss is an opportunity." Don't exit too soon on a small profit after a move starts; exit quickly when wrong.
- Too soon / too late: wait for a well-defined change in trend, then act. Refers to "45 Years in Wall Street" for many more rules.
- **Hope ruins; fear can save you if you act quickly when wrong** — "the fear of the market is the beginning of wisdom" (Gann's paraphrase of Proverbs).
- You cannot make the market go your way. Small losses are the expense of a successful speculator. **Never average a loss.** Speculation is a profession requiring training like law/medicine.
### Rules for trading in stocks (p.7)
1. **Buy at new high prices or old top levels.**
2. **Buy when prices advance above old low price levels.**
3. **Sell when prices decline below old top levels or high prices.**
4. **Sell at new low price levels.** Safer to wait for **1–2 points beyond** the level, and **still safer to wait for a CLOSE beyond it.**
5. **Closing prices:** in active fast markets use the daily chart and the **close** above highs/below lows; intraday pokes often reverse by the close. "The longer the time period in days, weeks, months or years when prices exceed old highs or break old lows, **the greater the importance** of the change in trend." New highs generally **react back to the old tops = safe buy**; breaks below old lows generally **rally back to the old lows = safe sell.**
6. Stops placed **when you make the trade**, not later.
7. **Never risk more than 10% of trading capital on one trade; after one or two losses reduce the trading unit.**
### The 50% rules (pp. 7–8, 20–21)
- **"The best buying point"**: a decline to **50% of the highest price at which the stock ever sold**; next strongest: **50% between the extreme low and extreme high.**
- **Selling level:** after being considerably below the 50% point, the **first** advance to it is a sell/short level, stop **3 points above** the 50% level (if daily/weekly show topping). Next: 50% between extreme low and high.
- Chrysler worked example: 1946 high 141½ → 50% = 70¾; 1949 low 44 buy point; Jan 1953 high 98½ ("we always expect selling just under 100"); range 44–98½ → 50% = 71¼; Jun 1953 low 70 just below → Aug rally only to 73, closed 71 → short; 1932 low 5 to 141½ → 50% = 73¼ — a **close above 73¼** ⇒ higher; Feb 1954 low 57 — close below 57 ⇒ lower, next old low 44.
- **Strongest / weakest position (p.20):** a stock that sold very high, stayed a long time low, then **crosses 50% of its highest price (or 50% of its extreme range) is very strong**. Breaking **50% of the highest price** = very weak (moderate rally, sometimes a final bottom); breaking **50% of the extreme range** = **weakest possible position**, much lower. Prices often hover at the 50% point for weeks/months; **once exceeded, reverse position.** Celanese 1951 high 58 → broke 29 (50%) Apr 1953 → never 2 pts higher → 16. Studebaker 43½ → broke 21¾ → never rallied 2 → 15.
- **100% advance ⇒ natural reaction** (United Aircraft up >100% from May 1952 in Apr 1954; total NYSE value up >100% from Jun 1949).
- **Round-number resistance:** "we always expect selling just under 100" (p.8).
### Stops (pp. 8–9)
- Stops go **below swing lows / above swing highs**, not merely below the daily low; for bigger moves, above old tops / below old bottoms **on the weekly or monthly chart**.
- Stops **under closing prices** (daily/weekly/monthly) are safer and caught less often than under intraday lows.
- **Stop distance by price level:** ~$10 stock → **1 point** below the close; $20–30 → **2 points**; $90–150 → **3 points** under lows/closing prices (above highs for shorts). ⇒ ≈ 10% → 7–10% → 2–3% — Gann's stop *shrinks as a percentage* as price rises. (GSPS equivalent: `EQUITY_STOP_MIN_PCT/MAX_PCT` band in `lib/strat/levels.ts` — Gann's schedule is price-tiered, not a flat % band; do **not** port points, but note the tiering principle.)
- At extreme high prices in very active markets, the **daily chart gives the first sign** of trend change, later confirmed weekly.
### What to do before a trade (p.9)
Check daily, weekly, monthly, yearly records and **all time periods**; note proximity to old highs/lows of recent weeks or years; **calculate the risk before the trade**; place the stop after entry.
### Timeframe significance (pp. 9–10)
- Crossing a **series of weekly highs/lows** ⇒ change lasting many weeks; **many months' highs/lows** ⇒ several months; **highs/lows several years back** ⇒ nearly always big, long moves. After crossing, expect a **reaction back to about the old high or slightly lower** (and rally back to broken lows). **"The greater the time period when it is exceeded, the greater the move."**
### Rules for stocks (p.10)
- **A.** Bull-market reactions: quick and sharp but **never more than 3–4 weeks** as a general rule ⇒ "stocks are a buy on a reaction of about 1 month in a bull market." Bear-market rallies **3–4 weeks, seldom more than one month**; after sharp severe declines rallies can last **2 months**; likewise a bull reaction can run 2 months after extremes. ⇒ guide to when the main trend is changing.
- **B.** Most money in the shortest time: follow the big money — buy when accumulation is complete and markets are ready to move fast; sell after distribution. Accumulation and distribution **take a long time**; the move after is fast. Markets go **up fastest after a long period** and **down fastest in the last stages** of a long decline.
- Worked examples (pp. 11–14): Boeing (buy new highs Sep 1950 at 37 → 56; Feb 1954 at 57 → 83 in 2 months), Douglas (Oct 1953 new high 74 → 133 in 6 months), Glenn Martin (1952 higher bottom 9; four months in a 2-pt range = support; Feb 1954 crossed 18½ → 25½ on 384 k shares week ending Apr 3 1954 = **public buying + short covering ⇒ reaction due** → reacted to 21¾ = old 1948/1951 highs), United Aircraft (double tops/bottoms; crossed 41½ Oct 1953 → 59½, the highest since Sep 1930 (65) — **64–65 next resistance**), Lockheed, Southern Railway, Vanadium (Mar 1954 new high 47 → 59¾ in 1 month).
- 1954: no manipulation/pools ⇒ moves come from investors; study harder.
### Total value of all NYSE stocks — the "Dollar Trend" (pp. 15–20)
- Gann charts the **monthly total market value** of all NYSE stocks, and of each group (aircraft, 39 steel & iron stocks), published by the NYSE Research Dept. — "a more important trend indicator than the average price" and **"much more accurate and valuable than the Dow-Jones 30 Industrial Average"** (pp. 25, 27).
- Same rules apply to the value line: new highs = buy; broken lows = sell; triple support = buy; one-month declines in uptrends; e.g. Mar 1938 low = **50% of Feb–Mar 1937 high** = buying level; May 1946 high 85 bn, 55 bn above Apr 1942 low ⇒ watch for change; **three months with no gain after a long advance ⇒ reaction due** (Dec 1952–Feb 1953); since Jun 1949 no reaction >5 bn ⇒ next could be ~10 bn (**2× the previous reaction**), which occurred.
- **Resistance projection (p.19):** 1929 high 90 bn **+50% = 135 bn** selling level; also ~325% of the Apr 1944 value ≈ 135 bn.
- **Time (p.19):** Apr 30 1954 = **12 years** from Apr 30 1942 low; Jun 30 1932 → Apr 30 1954 = **262 months**; many changes in trend historically in **April and May** ⇒ watch.
- **How much decline to expect:** use the largest prior declines in value (Feb 1937–Apr 1938: 31.17 bn; May 1946–1949: 21.79 bn) as a guide for the first decline.
### Swings vs. holding (p.21)
Chrysler 1932–1954: range 5→141 = 136 pts, but 10-pt-or-greater swings total **1,458 pts**; catching half = 729 pts ⇒ **trade the swings** (in and out, long and short) rather than hold for dividends.
### Paired long/short & trade logs (pp. 22–24)
- Long Douglas / short Celanese (Jul–Aug 1951 at ~48/52) → $10,600 on $5,200 capital in <3 years.
- Studebaker (short at a lower third top 35 below two tops at 36, stop 37; buy on triple bottom 26; short on break of Feb and May 1952 lows; add on break of Oct–Nov 1952 low; 50% break at 21¾ → 15) and Vanadium (buy after 6 months at 31, stop 29; sell double top 44½/45–45½; buy new highs 41, 47; **sell at 59¾ because Sep 1953 low 30½ + 50% ≈ 61 and record volume — but don't short since main trend still up**) → ~$11,500 on ~$3,500 in 3 years.
- Spread: Vanadium 30½ vs Studebaker 28 (Sep 1953) → 45 pts apart by Apr 1954 ⇒ buy strong, sell weak.
### Low-priced stocks in modern markets (p.24)
Without pools, institutions buy quality (GM, SONJ, AT&T, DuPont, GE, Westinghouse, Douglas, U.S. Steel, Bethlehem, Vanadium); public holds "cats and dogs" in hope ⇒ low-priced stocks will fall faster in the next bear market. **"The day will come when the great time cycle indicates a great depression"** and even blue chips get sold low.
### Aircraft & steel group value worked examples (pp. 25–27)
- Buy the stock in the strongest group position at the group's low (Douglas Apr 1952: declined 66½ → 52; **50% of 43½–66½ = 55; closed April at 57 ⇒ buy, stop 51**). Split stocks move slower (Bell, Lockheed).
- Steel group: lower tops/bottoms each month Sep 1951 → Apr 1952; Bethlehem Sep 1953 at 44½ "not 3 points below the old bottoms," buy, stop 43.
- Vanadium Apr 1954: high 59¾ on very large volume → sell out; must break 54 to indicate lower; close above 60 ⇒ next resistance **67–68**, then **76½ (Mar 1931 high)**.

## Ch. 2 — *Method for Forecasting the Stock Market* + *The Human Body* (pp. 45–67; signed **January 17, 1931**, 78 Wall Street)
### Charts & the weekly third-week rule (p.46)
- Keep **yearly, monthly, weekly and Overnight** charts.
- **Weekly two-week reversal rule:** a stock often reverses the minor trend for **two or three weeks**, and in the **third week does not make a higher top or higher bottom** (or holds several weeks without exceeding the first two weeks' extreme). **It is always safe to buy or sell with a stop 3 points above/below the two-week reverse move.** If the market is going on, it should continue in the **third week**. Same in bull and bear.
- **Overnight Chart** (see Ch. 3): to show a reversal it must go **3 points above the last top or below the last bottom**; it is **based on bottoms** — progressive (higher) bottoms = up, decreasing bottoms = down. "The basis of all movements are calculated from bottoms."
### How to forecast — philosophy (p.46)
- "Every movement in the market is the result of a natural law and of a **Cause which exists long before the Effect** takes place and can be determined years in advance." Future = repetition of the past (Eccl. 1:9 quoted). ⇒ **Cause and Effect** + **Rhythm**, in Gann's own words.
- "**Everything has a major and a minor**"; know the major cycle — most money is made in extreme fluctuations.
- **Great cycle 49–50 years**; "jubilee" years of extreme high/low prices lasting **5–7 years** at the end of the 50-year cycle.
- "7" a **fatal number**, "ruled by the planet Saturn, which brings contractions, depressions and panics"; **7×7 = 49** "the fatal evil year."
### The cycle hierarchy (pp. 47–48) — Gann's own ranking
- **"The most important time cycle is the 20-year cycle, or 240 months,"** and most stocks and averages work closer to it than any other. Its divisions: **5 yrs = ¼ (60 mo); 10 yrs = ½ (120 mo); 15 yrs = ¾ (180 mo); 7½ yrs = 90 mo** ("because 84⅜ [printed 84-5/8] is 15/16 of 90" — i.e. 90-month and its 15/16 point).
- **30-year cycle** "caused by the planet Saturn" (one revolution ≈ 30 yrs); Saturn "rules the products of the earth" ⇒ extremes in commodities ⇒ stocks. Gann repeats: **"The most important cycle of all is the 20-year cycle."**
- **10-year cycle:** extremes every 10 years; "stocks come out remarkably close on each even 10-year cycle."
- **Minor cycles 3 and 6 years; smallest 1 year — change often in the 10th or 11th month.**
- **Rapid markets run 6–7 weeks** then some minor reversal; or continue for months reacting only **two weeks**, resting 2–3 weeks, then resuming; often move right on in the **third week**. **Daily analogue: fast markets move only two days against the main trend and resume on the third day.**
### Proportions (p.48)
- In all movements use angles and calculate the **⅓, ⅔, ¼, ¾ points** of the major or minor move. **"One-half is the most important as it equals the 45° angle, which is the strongest and most fatal. The next in importance is ⅔, which would equal a triangle, or 120°."** ⇒ the retracement ranking **½ > ⅔ > (⅓, ¼, ¾)** is Gann's own (supports `lib/gann/retracement.ts` ranking).
- **"All rules based on Natural Law are applied the same to Time, Space and Volume charts."** (Correspondence — one rule across three dimensions.)
- **Three-to-four-section rule:** "All markets move in three to four sections. **The third or fourth movement up or down marks the culmination.**" A **reverse signal is always given before time expires**; the market may then make 2–3 swings into the same territory, slightly above/below the warning tops/bottoms = accumulation/distribution.
### Time rules for forecasting stocks (pp. 48–50)
- Stocks move in **10-year cycles worked out as 5-year cycles — 5 up, 5 down.**
- **Rule 1:** campaigns don't run more than **3 to 3½ years** one way without a 3–6-month or 1-year counter-move. Many culminate in the **23rd month**; watch months **23–24, 34–35, 41–42, 49–60, 67–72, 84–90**.
- **Rule 2:** bull campaign **5 years = 2 up, 1 down, 2 up**; end in the **59th–60th month** ("always watch for the change in the 59th month").
- **Rule 3:** bear cycle **5 years = 2 down, 1 up, 2 down.**
- **Rule 4/5:** **add 10 years to any top (bottom)** → next top (bottom) of the 10-year cycle, same kind of year, similar fluctuations.
- **Rule 6:** bear campaigns run out in **7-year cycles = 3 + 4 years** from any complete bottom (add 3 → next bottom; add 4 more → 7-year bottom).
- **Rule 7:** from a complete top add **3 → next top; +3 → second top; +4 → third & final top** of a 10-year cycle (3+3+4 = 10).
- **Rule 8:** **top + 5 years → next bottom; bottom + 5 years → next top** (1917 bottom → 1922 top; 1919 top → 1924 bottom). Campaigns never more than **two years in the same direction** (per rules 2–3).
- **Rule 9 — annual forecasts:** the coming year runs close to **10 years back** (1932 ≈ 1902, 1912, 1922); for full work check **30 years back** (1922 forecast: check 1892, 1902, 1912; closest 1912). The 10-year-back and 20-year cycles have most effect. **"Each stock works from its own base or from its own tops and bottoms."**
- **Rule 10 — extreme great cycles:** "There must always be a major and a minor, a lesser and a greater, **a positive and a negative**" ⇒ three moves in 10 years (tops 3, 3, 4 years apart); 5-year = 2 major + 1 minor; **smallest complete cycle 5 years; 10 a complete cycle; 5×10 = 50 greatest cycle** — extremes at the end. **"The number 7 is the basis of time"**: a panic/depression **every 7 years** (1907, 1917…), greater than the 3-year decline; 7×7 = 49 → extremes in the 49th–50th year; extremes also at the end of 30-year cycles. **Always count cycles from tops or bottoms.** ⇒ Polarity stated explicitly by Gann.
- **Rule 11 — monthly:** same as yearly: bottom **+3 months, then +4 (=7)** → minor bottoms/reaction points. Bull reactions may last only **2–3 weeks**, so a market can rise **12 months without breaking a monthly bottom**. Big swings: reactions **≤2 months, the third month up** ("two down and the third up"); bear rallies ≤2 months. **"Most moves run out in six to seven weeks"; 7×7 = 49 days "a fatal turning point."** In a bull year a stock may react 2–3 weeks, rest 3–4 weeks, then advance 6–7 more weeks. Always check whether the **big time limit has run out** before judging a reverse move.
- **Rule 12 — daily:** important daily changes every **7 and 10 days**; natural changes within a month around the **6–7th, 9–10th, 14–15th, 19–20th, 23–24th, 29–31st**. **Watch 30 days from the last top/bottom ("very important"), then 60, 90, 120 days; 180 days (6 months) very important; the 9th and 11th months** from tops/bottoms for minor and often major changes.
- **Timeframe hierarchy of reversals:** daily gives the first short change (runs **7–10 days**), weekly the next (runs **3–7 weeks**), monthly the strongest (**2–3 months or more**).
- **Yearly bottoms rule:** a stock making **higher bottoms each year for five years, then a lower bottom than the previous year** = reversal sign, may mark a long down cycle (mirror for lower tops).
### Instructions for forecasting (pp. 50–51)
1. **"Time is the most important of all factors and not until sufficient time has expired does any big move up or down start. The time factor will overbalance both space and volume. When time is up, space movement will start and big volume will begin… Time rules."** Always consider the annual forecast.
2. **Each stock works out its 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 60-year cycles from its own bottoms and tops**, regardless of others, even in the same group.
3. Monthly reversals after **2–3 months**; the change starts **in the third month**.
4. **Weekly:** declines of 2–3 (sometimes 4) weeks; reversal usually **in the third week**, runs ≤3 weeks against the trend. In a bull market: 2 (possibly 3) weeks' decline; **mid-third-week the trend turns up and closes higher at the end of the third week**; sometimes not until the **fourth week**, closing higher at its end. Reverse for bears.
5. **Rapid advances/declines on big volume run about seven weeks** = culmination moves; watch for top/bottom around the **49th–52nd day**, sometimes the **42nd–45th day**.
- At the end of any big move time must be consumed for accumulation/distribution. After a 7-week decline, 2–3 short sideways weeks then up (= monthly third-month rule).
- Daily counter-moves run **7–10 days**, weekly **3–4 weeks (extreme 6–7)**, monthly **3–4 months** — then resume the main trend; **"as a general rule, in a bear market stocks never rally more than two months. They start breaking in the third month."**
- **Retracement reversal test (p.51):** after extreme advances/declines, **the first time the market reverses over ¼ or ½ of the distance travelled in the last section, consider the trend turned at least for the present.** When time is running out, space shows the reversal by **breaking back over ¼, ⅓ or ½ of the last move.** Never decide the trend has changed without consulting the angles and the stock's position in its cycle.
### Geometric angles (pp. 51–56)
- Draw from bottom "0" upward and from top "T" downward. **First the 45°, then 2×1 on each side**; add 4×1/8×1 (and 1×4/1×8) only for fast moves. Pattern-chart scale **1 point per ⅛ inch**.
- **Rates (monthly chart):** to hold above the 45° a stock must raise its bottoms **1 pt/month**; 8×1 = 8 pts/month; 4×1 = 4; 3×1 = 3; 3×2 = 1½ pts/month (18/yr); **2×1 (the flatter side) = 6 pts/year = ½ pt/month**; flatter angles 4, 3, 2 pts/year; the 6×1-flat angle only 2 pts/yr — a stock that can't hold above such a flat angle is "on the bear side of the square." (The transcription marks left/right and 3×2 vs 2×3 as uncertain.)
- **Angles need not be drawn from far back — calculate them:** bottom 15 in Jan 1900 → 45° rises 1 pt/month → Jan 1930 (~360 mo) … Gann's example gives 120 months ⇒ 135 (text says "20 years later… 10 years would be 120 months… 135 in January 1930"; internal inconsistency in the source — the arithmetic is bottom + months elapsed).
- **Angle degrees on the Pattern Chart:** 3¾°, 7½°, 15°, 18¾°, 26¼° [printed 26½], 30°, 33¾°, 37½°, 45°, 52½°, 56¼°, 60°, 63¾°, 71¼°, 75°, 82½°, 86¼°, 90° (these are 1/24ths and 1/16ths of 90°).
- **Proof by crossing:** 8×1 up from 0 and 8×1 down from 90 cross on the 45 line at 5⅝ over; 4×1 pair crosses at 11¼ — because 45 is ½ of 90, **parallel angles from 0 up and 90 down meet at the "gravity center."**
- **From any bottom two 45° angles** (up and down from the vertical); likewise from any top. Worked: U.S. Steel low 150 on Nov 13 1929 → 45° down 1 pt/month → Nov 1930 low 138 on it (12 months = 12 points); Dec 1930 extreme 134⅜, 2 pts under, but **resting on the 2×1 from the Jan 1927 low 111½**; closed back above the 45°.
- **Break of the 45° (daily/weekly/monthly) = very weak, expect the next angle; regaining it = stronger.** Re-crossing reverses the trend again. **Monthly and weekly angles matter more than daily.**
- **Distance from base:** "the farther away from the base, the more important the change in trend" when an angle is broken or crossed.
- **Each stock works out its own square** by its extreme high/low or the square of its tops: U.S. Rubber total high 143 → move over **143 months** (11 yrs 11 mo) → ends Dec 1931 = change-in-trend point; divide the square into ¼, ½, ¾, ⅓, ⅔.
### Why angles work — the circle (pp. 53–54)
- Strength order: **90° (strongest — vertical/horizontal)**, then **180°**, **270°**, **360°**; then **120° and 240°** (⅓ and ⅔ of the circle — "especially strong for measurements of time"); then **45°, 135°, 225°, 315°**. **Cardinal Cross = 90/180/270/360; Fixed Cross = 45/135/225/315** — "very important for the measurements of time and space and volume."
- 22½° stronger than 11¼°; **67½° = 1½×45** strong on the way to 90; **78¾° = ⅞ of 90** one of the strongest before 90 — **many stocks top/bottom around the 78th–80th week, month or day.**
- **Why ⅛ divisions dominate:** money is divided in ¼, ½, ⅛ ($1 = 4 quarters; old 12½¢ pieces); stock prices were quoted in eighths ⇒ price/time work out closer to eighths than thirds. **Par $100 → degrees: 12½ = 45°, 25 = 90°, 37½ = 135°, 50 = 180°, 62½ = 225°, 75 = 270°, 87½ = 315° [printed 82½], 100 = 360°.** A stock at 50 on the 180th day/week/month is "on the degree of its time angle" (price = time). U.S. Steel Feb 1 1915 low 38 ≈ 37½ (⅜ of 100 = 135°) when Steel was **168 months (14 yrs) old** ≈ 135° angle — "behind time" but strong. Crossing 200 = 2 circles; 261¾ ≈ 262½ = ⅝ of the third hundred = 225°.
- **Master Timing Angles** (referenced, instructions not in this chapter): what happens when each timing angle reaches **24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, 90, 120, 135, 157½, 180, 210, 225, 240, 270, 300, 315, 330, 345, 360 months** from incorporation; timing angles #5–9 crossing others; returns to the same degree = "exact measurements of cycles according to moving energy," computed from **U.S. Government astronomers' figures** (i.e. planetary ephemerides) out to 2000. *(Astrological mechanism — see AGENTS.md astrology decision: non-gating only, and only with a cited method.)*
### How to use geometric angles (pp. 55–56)
- "My method… is all based on mathematical points or geometrical angles." **Resistance levels are ⅛, ¼, ½, ¾, ⅓, ⅔… proportional parts of a circle** ⇒ geometric angles.
- Three factors: **price, time, space movements**; fourth: **volume at tops/bottoms**.
- Price 45 meets resistance (= 45°). Breaking a 45° angle anywhere weakens; **more important far from the base.** A stock resting on the 45° **three times** in successive reactions then **breaking it the fourth time** is in an extremely weak position (far from base, much time elapsed).
- Daily charts can break/recover angles; a daily 45° can hold a very long time **except in the final grand rush** at the end of big bull campaigns.
- **Time-and-space balance:** breaking the 45° at price **135 in the 135th month** = strong resistance level + strong angle + strong time angle ⇒ big decline. (Price = time coincidence at a strong degree.)
- Volume: sales increase near tops, decrease near bottoms; long liquidation ends on shrinking volume.
### Volume charting — the "space by volume" method (pp. 56–60)
- **Volume chart:** move one ⅛-inch space per **25,000 shares** (Vanadium) instead of per week ⇒ angles drawn on a **volume-time axis**. Worked Vanadium weekly Sep 1928–Jun 1930 with weekly volumes: breakouts on volume expansions; tops on climactic weeks (238,900; 175,000; 206,000; 258,100 ≈ **"volume almost equalling the total amount of stock outstanding" = plain top**); bottoms on dry-ups (8,800 shares Aug 31 1929 = no stock for sale; 12–19 k at Dec 1929 bottom after low 37½ on Nov 13 1929).
- **Halfway point overshoot allowance:** from 37½ to 143¼, ½ = 90⅜; the decline went **3⅜ beyond**, due to volume/momentum — **"often a stock will go 3¾ points beyond the half-way point and then recover, especially after a big decline. The greater the decline in points, the more allowance… around the main center."** Thin-float stocks make faster, wider moves. ⇒ Gann's own tolerance band around 50% (cf. `lib/gann/entryTrigger.ts` lost-motion; `lib/gann/retracement.ts`).
- **Confluence at 125 (p.58):** ⅔ of the 143½–87 swing = 126¾; ⅞ of the total value 143¼ = 125⅜; and 25 = ¼ of 100 ⇒ **"a very strong resistance level"** (multiple proportional levels coinciding).
- After a two-week decline wiping out half the advance on ~⅓ of the whole advance's volume ⇒ "the decline was too fast according to time" ⇒ rally due (rally expectation **⅓ to ½ of the decline**).
- Dow monthly volume 1921–1930: Jun 1928 → Sep 1929 (194 → 381) never more than **one month's reaction**, never broke **10 points under a previous month's bottom**; Oct 1929 first break of a prior month's low since May 1929 ⇒ trend down. Nov 1929–Apr 1930 = bear-market rally (111 M in April on tiny price gain), rallying up **under the 1929 heavy-volume distribution zone.** Chart scale 2 pts per ⅛ inch **doubles angle values** (2×1 behaves as 45°) — **angle meaning depends on the price-time scale** (a scaling choice Gann makes explicit). 248 = crossing of two angles (from Nov 1929 and from Oct 1919) = important point; Jan 1930 low 246.
### Fast advances/declines & the "square of distance" (pp. 60–62)
- Fast advances reverse sharply before breaking the 67½°/45° on monthly/weekly charts because **the volume chart breaks the angle first** (large volume moves the "space" axis faster than calendar time). ⇒ **volume-time is the leading axis at climaxes.**
- **Square of distance:** after a 100-pt decline in 2–3 months, **"to overcome the square of distance at a low level, it requires 100 months"** — a stock must "square itself" on weekly, monthly and daily charts. **The lower the price at which it crosses the 45°, the stronger; the higher/farther from base when an angle is broken, the weaker.**
- First crossing of the 45° often comes **before** the square of distance is overcome ⇒ secondary reaction rests on the 45° **when the square of distance is reached**, then a greater advance. Mirror at tops: quick decline, then slightly higher or series of slightly lower tops, until the square is overcome at a high level, the 45° breaks and a fast decline follows.
- **Draw angles from each higher bottom** (and each lower top); **the second higher bottom / second lower top is always very important** to draw angles and measure time from.
- **When in doubt, don't trade.** Wait for a break/cross of a resistance level or important angle, especially after a long deadlock; important trend changes show in volume (volume rises as a stock starts from dullness, and when a high-level dull period breaks down). **Important angles cannot be crossed at extreme lows, or broken at highs, until proper time has elapsed ⇒ an angle break usually means time has run out.**

## Ch. 3 — *Method for Trading with the Overnight Chart — Mechanical Stock Trading Method* (pp. 68–116; 91 Wall Street; log to ~Mar 1931)
**"Purely mechanical. You use no judgement but simply follow rules and reverse your position when the Overnight Chart indicates it."** — the most directly codeable system in Gann's corpus.

### Construction of the Overnight Chart (p.68) — algorithm
Input: daily high/low. State: current direction, current chart level, last recorded top/bottom.
1. **While each day makes a higher bottom, move the chart up** (to that day's high — the chart line follows the up-swing).
2. **The first day the low is ≥ ¼ point under the previous day's bottom, move the chart down** to that low, **recording the highest top reached before the turn.**
3. While it makes lower bottoms, keep moving it down.
4. **A higher bottom and a lower top on the same day (inside day) → move it up to that day's top** ("because the Overnight Chart is based on bottoms").
5. **Outside day** (higher top, then lower bottom): first move up to the high, **then** down to the low. Next day a higher bottom → move up to that day's top.
⇒ This is a **one-bar swing chart keyed on the lows** (a ¼-pt filter on the break of the prior low). It differs from `lib/gann/swingChart.ts` (3-day/9-day, close-count based). *Implementation note:* modern equivalent of "¼ point" = a minimum tick/ATR filter; Gann's 3-point reversal (Ch. 2 p.46) is the larger-swing variant.

### Trading rules (pp. 68–71)
- Use only the **half-way point** ("gravity center": extreme low + extreme high of the last move ÷ 2) plus the Overnight Chart in the worked log: **buy/sell when the half-way point is reached, 1-point stop.** Where no resistance levels exist (new highs), just follow the Overnight Chart. Watch daily highs/lows near Permanent Resistance Levels.
- **Rule 1:** buy/sell **double or triple tops/bottoms** with a stop **1 point** beyond (a 3-pt stop often makes more but is caught less… Gann uses 1 pt; when caught, reverse). **Greatest moves start from triple tops/bottoms several weeks or months apart**; ones only days apart mean smaller moves.
- **Rule 2:** **the 4th time at the same top/bottom it nearly always goes through** — use a 1-pt stop when trading the 4th test.
- **Rule 3:** Overnight tops/bottoms **at ½, ⅔, ¾ resistance levels** → buy/sell with a stop 1–2 (generally 1) point beyond the exact level.
- **Rule 4:** **when the stop is caught the Overnight Chart has reversed — reverse position and "double up"** (i.e. the stop order both exits and opens the opposite position of the same unit; "sell out longs… go short the same amount"). Exception: don't reverse where there's no nearby second top/bottom to place the new stop against. Stops generally **1 point beyond a previous top/bottom.**
- **Rule 5 — pyramiding:** add **half the trading unit every 3–5 points** (distance by activity and resistance levels broken); **3rd lot = ½ of 2nd, 4th = ½ of 3rd…** — largest risk first. With a large profit in a sharp move: trail the stop **5 points from the day's extreme**; out on the first 5-pt reverse move.
- **Rule 6:** very active, fast stocks at high levels → wait for an actual Overnight Chart change (break of a previous low/high) before reversing.
- **Rule 7:** after any big move, reverse on an Overnight change ("if it is time to sell out longs, it is also time to go short"). In panics (Oct–Nov 1929) with big pyramid profits, trail **~10 points** from the market, then **~5 points** above the low after a severe decline — **don't wait for the Overnight Chart** in such fast markets. Also stop at **important half-way points** (U.S. Steel 150 = ½ of 38→261¾: cover and buy at 150 with stop 149, or 147 by the 3-point rule).
- **Rule 8:** when an active stock **closes for 3 days, 3 weeks or 3 months around the same price**, then the trend changes, it usually goes a considerable distance in the new direction (supplementary, not required for the Overnight method).
- **Rule 9 — "3 full points":** in very weak/strong markets watch for the **first 3-full-point move from any extreme**, counted on **even figures** (low 99½ → needs 103, not 102½; high 150⅞ → reaction to 147 counts, 147¼ does not) ⇒ minor trend reversing.
- **Risk:** never risk >3 points on an initial trade; thereafter 1-pt Overnight stops; **$3,000 per 100 shares** ($300 → 10-share lots, ≤$30 risk); don't raise the unit until a loss would be ≤10% of capital; **pyramid stops set so a full stop-out loses ≤10% of capital.** "Use as little human judgment as possible… **You must have machine-like action.**"
- Gann states he deliberately makes known-losing trades in the log "just to demonstrate that the Method will work over a long period of years."

### U.S. Steel worked log, Feb 1915 → Jun 1930 (pp. 72–116) — characteristics
- Start **$3,000, 100 shares** (Feb 1 1915 low 38; entry on the first **3-point rally** to 41½, buy 41). Trading unit stepped **100 → 200 → 300 → 700 → 1,000 → 1,200 → 1,500 → 2,000 → 5,000** as capital grew (≈ $3,000 of capital per 100 shares, then "ultra conservative" once large — at $121 k Gann notes **ten consecutive 3-pt losses would still leave half the capital**).
- Claimed result: **$5,957,511 net by Jun 7 1930** (after an explicit commission/tax **"ERROR" correction of $162,191** he inserts himself), ~$6.02 M by Jun 30 1930. Treat as **illustrative, hindsight paper trading** — not evidence of expectancy (he chooses the half-way levels and some discretionary exits).
- Recurrent decision points in the log (each a codeable rule): (a) **1 pt beyond the last Overnight bottom/top** as the reversing stop; (b) **stop 1–2 pts under two/three bottoms close together**; (c) **cover and reverse at the ½ point of the last swing** with a 1-pt (or 3-pt) stop (e.g. 54½ = ½ of 60¾–48¼; 67⅞; 82⅛; 107¾; 114⅞; 97; 96⅝; 88⅝; 106⅜; 104⅛; 205¾; 234⅛); (d) **new-high breakouts after long ranges** (Aug 1916: +1 pt into new high after **nine months between 89½ and 79¾**) start new pyramids; (e) **round-number resistance 100** ("can't go thru 100… sell at 99"; later buy at 100½ once through); **120, 150 as resistance levels**; (f) **the long-term halves**: 150 = ½ of 38→261¾ (and 1928 move start 149¾ ⇒ "strongest support"), 166⅛ = ½ of 70½ (1921) → 261¾, 135 = ½ of 8⅜ (life low) → 261¾ "half of the life fluctuation", 169 = **⅜** of 198¾→151⅝ (a ⅜ level used as an exit); (g) **"two years from the low"** — Feb 1 1917 is exactly 2 years from Feb 1 1915 low 38 (time anniversary noted in the log); (h) panic days: **pull stops 5 pts from the low**; **fast double-pyramid: stop 10 pts under each day's high** (Aug–Sep 1929: sold all longs at 251¾ on Sep 3 1929 — the top day was Sep 3, high 261¾); (i) **"failing to get 3 points above the ½ point"** → exit (Feb 1931, 152¼ vs ½ point); (j) crossing 152–153 → "very strong position."

### Implementation relevance for GSPS
- The **Overnight Chart** is a distinct Gann swing definition (bar-low keyed, ¼-pt filter, inside/outside-day handling) not present in `lib/gann/`. It is a candidate *second, finer swing definition* next to `swingChart.ts` — and it is the swing definition Gann himself used for **stops** (1 pt beyond the last Overnight bottom). `lib/strat/levels.ts#nearestStructuralStop` uses structural pivots; the Overnight rule is the Gann-specific version.
- **Stop-and-reverse** is Gann's own default in this method; GSPS does not reverse. Relevant to Strategy/automation design only if a "Gann mechanical" mode is ever wanted — note that AGENTS.md says Gann is the default *verdict*, and this is literally Gann's mechanical system.
- **Pyramiding with halving units** and **trailing 5/10 pts from the extreme in fast markets** — no GSPS equivalent.
- **Half-way-point reversal trades with 1-pt stops** — `lib/gann/retracement.ts` supplies the level; the *trade rule* (enter at the ½ of the last swing, stop 1 pt beyond) is not in GSPS entries.
