## Content read (synthesis, with page references)

### Why commodities; the foundation (front matter, Ch. I, pp. 1–29)
- 18 reasons commodities beat stocks: seasonal trend, fewer charts, no cross-currents, crops recur every year.
- The five qualifications (knowledge, patience, nerve, health, capital) and "never buck the trend."
- **Series-of-losses rule.** After 1–3 losing trades "something is wrong with you and not with the market." Get out, wait and study (p. 12). "When a man's trend changes" (p. 17): after a 2nd or 3rd loss your personal trend has changed, so stop; after 4th/5th losses, rest. **Rule 27: reduce trading after the first loss; never increase.** Capital rule (p. 29): **after 3 consecutive losses, reduce the unit to 10% of *remaining* capital.**
- **Wars and booms every 20–25 years** because a new, inexperienced generation arrives. History repeats because human nature does not change (p. 15).
- **Popular trading prices:** the public thinks in 5s and 10s (25, 50, 75, 100, 150), so markets turn just short of round numbers. Don't place limit orders; trade at the market (p. 19).
- News is discounted. When the market *fails* to rise on good news (or fall on bad), something is wrong (p. 8). "Coming events cast their shadows before" (p. 21).
- Normal reactions grow with price: 5–6¢, then 10–12¢, then 15–20¢. Over 15–20¢ means the market is turning abnormal (p. 18).
- Creeping markets end in a "grand rush" (p. 19). Reactions are smaller at high levels and rallies smaller at low levels (p. 23). Strongest position: the 3rd/4th higher bottom (p. 25).

### Chart construction and levels (Ch. II, pp. 29–55)
- **Capital:** $3,000 per 10,000 bu; stop ≤ 3¢; risk ≤ 10% per trade.
- **Charts:** weekly and monthly for the busy trader. **Quarterly (seasonal) chart**: the first break of a prior quarter's low after a long advance, or the first cross of a prior quarter's high after a long decline, signals a change of main trend. Daily chart only in very active, final-stage markets (pp. 30–31).
- **Percentages of the *low price*** (divide by 8): 12½, 25, 33⅓, 37½, **50**, 62½, 66⅔, **75**, 87½, **100**%. **50% and 100% of a bottom are the two most important** (p. 32). For cotton-type markets, 10% multiples (p. 33).
- **Measuring stick:** after the final bottom, take the % of the first secondary reaction; the first reaction exceeding it signals a trend change (p. 33).
- **Time is still primary:** "the first time the time period exceeds the greatest time period" of prior counter-moves, the trend has changed (p. 33).
- **Range divisions:** eighths, then thirds. Also divide the **highest selling price** by 8 and by 3 (p. 34). Importance order:
  1. the range between the all-time extreme high and low
  2. divisions of the highest price
  3. each campaign of a year or more
  4. 3rd/4th lower tops
  - The 50% "gravity center" is the balancing point. "You can make a fortune by following this one rule alone" (p. 36).
- **Secondary bottoms and tops:** the 50% point between the first rally's top and the secondary reaction's bottom is strong support (mirror for tops) (p. 35).
- **Resistance points near the same level: add them and divide by 2** (p. 37). **Resistance Level Card:** the more levels near a price, the stronger it is (p. 37).
- **Lost motion averages 1⅞¢; price will go 1⅞¢ past a level but not 3 full cents**, so a 3¢ stop is caught least often. The gravity-center analogy (p. 38).
- **Nine buying points and nine selling points** (pp. 39–41). These include:
  - old tops and bottoms (the 4th test is dangerous)
  - crossing a series of weekly tops
  - a secondary reaction after the advance **exceeds the greatest rally of the preceding decline**
  - the first rally **exceeding in time** the greatest rally of the prior bear campaign
  - breakaway points
  - 50% of the highest price or of the range
  - double/triple bottoms
  - 2-day reactions in final fast stages, following the previous day's low with a stop
- **Tops and bottoms are swing extremes**, not arbitrary bar highs and lows (p. 41).
- **Triple tops/bottoms years apart are the most important.** A **4th test** usually goes through; if it holds, a big reversal follows (p. 43).
- **28 Valuable Rules** (p. 43): the 24 stock rules adapted, plus #25 "let the market prove it is top/bottom," #26 on advice, **#27 reduce after the first loss**, #28 double mistakes.
- **Formations** (pp. 48–50): single, **U** (flat, 3–10 weeks), **W** (double), **WV** (triple), **WW** (4-bottom); **A** (sharp top after 17–26 weeks), **U** top, **M**, **MA**, **MM**. **Breakaway points** are where the fast move starts after time has run out.
- **Sections:** bull and bear campaigns run 3–4 sections (up to 7 in extreme war years); minor campaigns under 1 year run 2 (p. 50).
- **Space and time rules for change of main trend** (p. 51):
  - after 3–4 sections, a decline exceeding the greatest prior reaction (in cents) signals a change
  - a reaction exceeding the greatest prior reaction **in time** (e.g. more than 4 weeks) signals a change
  - the signal matters more after the 3rd/4th section than after the 2nd
- **Many weeks in a narrow range:** 2–6 or 10–13 weeks. The longer the range, the bigger the breakout.
- **Range of bottoms/tops:** 1–3¢ is a double/triple; 3 full cents through signals continuation. After crossing old tops, 1–2¢ back under is normal and 5¢ is a reversal (p. 53). Moves are faster at high prices (public trades low prices; professionals above $2) (p. 54).

### Forecasting (Ch. III, pp. 56–64)
- **"TIME… will overbalance both Space and Volume. When TIME is up, space movement will start and large volume will begin."** (p. 56)
- **Yearly:** higher bottoms for 5 years, then a lower one, signals a reversal. **Monthly:** add 3 months, then 4 (7), to a bottom for minor bottoms. In bull markets reactions rarely last past 2 months ("2 down, 3rd up"). Grains rarely rally more than 2 months in a bear market.
- **Weekly:** bull-market counter-moves run 2–3 (sometimes 4) weeks and turn mid-3rd week. Fast moves run 6–7 weeks, extreme ones 13–15 weeks (¼ year).
- **Day counts:**
  - **7 × 7 = 49 days: watch days 49–52**, and 42–45 (45 = ⅛ year)
  - **90–98 and 120–135 days**
  - **30, 60, 90, 120, 180 (most important), 270 and 330 days** from tops and bottoms
  - **monthly change days: 6–7, 9–10, 14–15, 19–20, 23–24, 29–31**
- **January rule:** the high and low of Jan 2–7 and 15–21 hold for months. Early-January lows often aren't broken until Jul/Aug or all year (p. 58).
- **Anniversary dates:** 1, 2, 3 years from extreme highs and lows. Within a year watch months 3–4, 6–7, 10–11. Most important at **12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48 months** (p. 58).
- **Year divisions:** ½ = 26 weeks, ¼ = 13 weeks, ⅓ = 17⅓ weeks, ⅛ = 45 days / ~6–7 weeks, **1/16 = 22½ days ≈ 3 weeks** (why 3-week moves are common). A 4th consecutive higher weekly close means continuation. The "5th… of Ascension" is Gann's numerological phrasing, **not admissible**.
- **Minor trend change:** 2+ weeks of tops at the same level, then a break under the 2-week bottom. Dull narrow markets precede breakouts.
- **Bull reactions** last 3–4 weeks and close higher in the 4th; extreme ones 6–7 weeks. **Bear rallies** last 2–4 weeks; one holding into the 5th week likely runs 6–7.
- **Signal (reversal) day, p. 60:** after a big advance, a day that opens high, makes a new high, then breaks under the open and **closes near the low**. Same on the weekly bar. Wider range plus an extreme close means a stronger signal.
- **Weekly open/close rule (p. 60):** at an old top or late section, a week that doesn't trade more than ~1 point above Monday's open and **closes near the week's low** is the first sign of change (mirror at bottoms). The weekly chart with open and close is "one of the best."
- **"Wait to sell until it boils"** (p. 61): bull campaigns end in a fast 6–7 (up to 10) week advance on huge volume. A 6–7 week panicky decline on heavy volume ends bear campaigns.
- **New territory** (p. 62): the longer the time before breaking into new ground, the bigger the move ("accumulated energy"; the dam analogy). Watch 7, 10–12, 25–36, 40–45¢ into new ground. In the 3rd/4th section, new highs don't run far.
- **Volume, four rules (p. 63):**
  1. large volume marks the end of a bull campaign; a secondary rally on falling volume confirms the top
  2. after a 2nd lower top and a dull range, a breakout on rising volume means a further decline
  3. **at bottoms, volume decreases and the range narrows; that is liquidation running its course**
  4. a secondary reaction on falling volume, then an advance on rising volume, means higher
  - **Summary: "Sales increase near the top and decrease near the bottom, except in abnormal markets like Oct–Nov 1929"** (panic bottoms on heavy volume).
- **Open interest** (p. 64): peak open interest before the price high, while volume rises, means the top is near. Rising open interest before the final low means good buying.

## Content read in the second pass (pp. 61–328, synthesis)

### Ch. III completion: volume and open interest worked through (pp. 61–67)
- **Rule 1 applied (p. 66).** A secondary rally on volume about 50% below the top's volume marks the public as "loaded", making the market a safe short sale on decreasing volume. Wheat 1939–41 daily volumes are worked as examples: the largest volume comes a few days *before* the final top, and volume drops on the day of the extreme.
- **Panic day (p. 66).** A limit-down day on the largest volume in weeks marked a bottom for a rally. A smaller-volume secondary decline then confirmed that "liquidation had run its course".
- **Open interest (pp. 66–67).** It peaks before the price high and falls while the price still rises (distribution), and it rises before the final low (accumulation). Open interest was lower in 1941 because of regulation, so its level is context-dependent. Watch the change, not the level.

### Ch. IV: time periods, resistance levels, the wheat record 1841–1941 (pp. 68–126)
- **Natural resistance points (p. 68).** The dollar divided in 8ths ("bits": 12½, 25, 37½, 50 …) and 16ths (6¼¢ steps), because the public prices in these units. Also "even figures" (30, 40, 45, 55 … 120). The 1841–1941 walk-through tests every turn against these.
- **Resistance from percentages of prior extremes (pp. 69–92).** Every major top or bottom is explained by several coinciding levels:
  - ⅛s and ⅓s of the **highest price**
  - ⅛s and ⅓s of **the range** between extremes, including secondary tops and bottoms
  - **percentage advances on the base low**: 50/100/150/200 … % added to the lowest price (28¢ in 1852 → 56, 84, 112, 140, 168 …). The 1855 top at 170 was a 500% advance on 28, and the 1937 top of 145 matched 1/3 of $3.50 and ⅝ of 43⅛–325.
  - **½ and ¼ of the base** added to any top or bottom (14¢ and 7¢ on a 28¢ base).
  - **Rule restated (p. 99): "All moves … obey a natural mathematical law."** Normal reactions are 5–7¢ and 11–12¢; abnormal ones are 24, 36 and 48¢ (multiples of 12). Add the natural percentage points to any bottom and subtract them from any top. Watch ⅓ and ⅔ of any move, and ⅟₁₆-dollar steps.
- **Trading rules scattered in the walk-through:**
  - After a sharp decline, **expect a 50% rally** of it, and sell again if the rally stalls at old levels (p. 92).
  - **The first 25% (or 12½%) retracement of the last advance is the first sign of weakness** (pp. 85, 169, 175).
  - **Crossing the highs of two previous quarters turns the main trend up**, and breaking two quarterly bottoms turns it down (pp. 75, 77–78, 148). The quarterly chart "gives the most important change in trend" because a quarter is a season (p. 159).
  - Stay with the trend "as long as it is moving in your favour", following it with a stop (p. 61).
  - "**Old tops become bottoms and old bottoms become tops**", and history repeats because human nature does not change (p. 96).
  - Corners (squeezes) always break back quickly (p. 74). **"War is always bullish" on commodities** (p. 79 and throughout). This is a regime observation, not a rule to port to equities.
- **Seasonal extreme-month counts, wheat 1841–1941 (pp. 93–94).**
  - Lows: Aug 16, Apr 14, Oct 13, Mar 12, Dec 12, Nov 10, Jun 9, Sep 8, Feb 7, Jul 6, May 5, Jan 2.
  - Highs: May 19, Jan 15, Apr 11, Oct 11, Dec 11, Aug 10, Sep 9, Jul 7, Feb 6, Jun 2, Nov 2, Mar 1.
  - A rapid **August advance against the seasonal trend** marks a reversal (p. 88).
- **Great swings and time balancing (pp. 97–99).** Gann measures each great swing in months and **adds that period forward** from the turn to project the next one ("balancing previous time periods"). Examples: 180 months 1852→1867 projects to 1882; 242 months projects to Aug 1907; 76 months projects to May 1901.
- **Percentages of time (p. 99), the clearest statement anywhere in the book.** A **300-month (25-year) period** 1895→1919 is divided like a price range:
  - **50% of the time → June 1932 low**
  - **75% → Sept 1938 low**
  - **+36 months → Sept 1941 high**
  - full period → Dec 1944 to watch
  - "**Percentages of time periods work out the same as percentages of the price** at tops and bottoms."
  - The silk chapter (p. 293) repeats this with 12½/25/50/75/100% of time periods.
- **Main top to main top, bottom to bottom (pp. 108–109).**
  - Recurring intervals: 24, 31, 36, 45–49 and 60 months, plus 111–135 and 247–257 months.
  - **"Many important tops and bottoms coming out 36 months apart"** (p. 108).
- **Monthly move-length statistics, wheat 100 years (pp. 102–103).** A "move" = consecutive months without breaking the prior month's low (up) or high (down).
  - 10 months: 2 times; 9 months: 4; 7–8 months: 22; 5–6 months: 47; 3–4 months: 106; 2 months: 107; 1 month: 120.
  - So **watch for a turn at 5–6 months, more so at 3–4, and expect a top once a run reaches 9–10 months**.
  - **Bull markets react at most 2 months, and bear rallies last at most 2 months.** Natural reactions run **28–36 days**. Buy the 28–36-day reaction in a bull market and sell the rally in a bear market.
- **Cross-series.**
  - All grains follow wheat's general trend (p. 100). The **wheat–corn spread** tells which to trade: corn gaining on wheat means corn is the better buy (pp. 100–101).
  - Rye (p. 161), barley (p. 181) and wool (p. 304) "follow the trend of wheat [or cotton]".
  - This is Dewey's **cross-series clustering** stated as a trading rule.

### Soy beans, corn, rye, oats, barley, lard (pp. 127–190)
- **Seasonal tables for every market** (months of the year's highs and lows): soy beans 1913–41, corn 1859–1941, rye 1896–1941, oats 1861–1941, lard 1869–1941. Each chapter derives from its table the months to buy and the months to sell.
- **The seasonal rule of three (pp. 144, 160–161, 244).** "Seldom … more than 3 times in the normal seasonal months before there is a change or reversal":
  - after highs in the same month for 3 consecutive years, the 4th year runs opposite (sometimes the 3rd)
  - "When a market runs opposite the seasonal trend, then it runs opposite the next seasonal trend" (p. 170)
  - "The market seldom makes more than two tops opposite the seasonal trend" (p. 198)
- **Change windows from any extreme.** "**Watch for change in trend in the sixth or seventh month and at the end of each year**" (pp. 132, 144, 175). Also watch 1, 2 and 3 years from any top or bottom (p. 132), and five years from a high (p. 159).
- **Worked soy bean campaign, Aug 1940–Oct 1941 (pp. 134–137).**
  - Buy at a triple bottom with a stop 1–3¢ under it, and raise stops under each new bottom.
  - Pyramid on each crossing of old tops.
  - In a **runaway market, trail the stop 1¢ under the previous day's low**.
  - Never risk more than 10% of capital per trade.
  - Gann's own caveat (p. 134): the paper result is not what "any average human being" would achieve, because of "the HUMAN ELEMENT", hope and fear.
- **The breakaway point and the run (pp. 138, 140, 198).** Crossing the tops of several weeks or months after a long narrow range starts "the runaway move [that] always follows".
- **Fourth time at the same level "nearly always go[es] through"** (pp. 151, 199), confirming G15.
- **Triple bottoms three years apart give the greatest advances** (p. 130). "The further away in time old tops and bottoms are, the more important they are" (p. 214).
- **Normal reaction benchmarks (pp. 138, 140, 142).**
  - A reaction of **12½%** is normal in price.
  - **3–6 weeks** is normal in time. Soy bean reactions of 10 days or 2 weeks were "normal".
- **Crossing $1.00 the second or third time** is followed by much higher prices (p. 142), a round-number rule.
- **Never trade the current option in its delivery month** (p. 138).
- **Supply–demand and psychology (pp. 184–185, 190).**
  - Price is "the balancing of buying and selling power".
  - "Prices are never so low but what they can go lower as long as the trend is down, and never so high but what they can go higher as long as the trend is up."
  - **Tops are sharp and brief, and bottoms are long and narrow**, because fear keeps buyers away.
  - **The lower prices get, the smaller the rallies** (and the mirror at highs) until the market is oversold (p. 188).
  - Each new generation repeats the same mistakes.

### Cotton and cotton seed oil (pp. 190–241)
- **Cotton seasonal counts, 125 years (p. 191).**
  - Lows: Dec 31, Jan 27, Feb 12, Sep 11, Oct 11 …
  - Highs: Jan 19, Dec 19, Sep 15, Mar 14, Oct 14 …
  - **"SEPTEMBER IS THE MOST IMPORTANT MONTH IN THE YEAR FOR A CHANGE IN TREND"**, because of the September crop report.
- **Cotton time rule (p. 192), the most complete single statement of Gann's change windows in the book.** Watch for a change in trend:
  - in the **3rd or 4th month** from any important bottom or top (the faster the move, the surer)
  - then the **6th or 7th**
  - then **12, 24, 36 months**
  - also **15, 18 and 21 months** (1¼, 1½, 1¾ years)
  - "apply the same percentage of time to greater time periods"
  - The same list recurs for rubber (p. 288), hides (p. 275), eggs (p. 270) and silk (p. 293). Coffee adds "the fifteenth month … 125% taking twelve months as 100%" (p. 268).
- **Time over-balances price (p. 193).** "DECLINES or ADVANCES do not start until time is up or the market reaches the month for a change in trend."
- **Base-percentage ladder (pp. 192–193).**
  - With 5¢ cotton as the base, levels come every 25% of the base.
  - "**50% advance on any bottom or any top is the most important point, then 100% advance on any bottom**, then 100% on any top."
- **Fast markets, daily rules (p. 200), a direct precursor of the 1951 swing chart.**
  - Keep a **30-point swing chart** in very active cotton. The first break under a 30-point bottom signals the change, and crossing a 30-point top signals higher.
  - In a run where **reactions never exceed 2 days**, the first 3-day reaction (or a second lower close) signals a change. The mirror applies in declines.
  - p. 215 applies the same rule in **points and days together**: "no reaction … more than two days and … more than 60 points", so the first break exceeding both marks the turn. This is over-balancing time *and* price on the daily chart.
- **Signal day worked (p. 216).** Sept 12 1941: a new high, then a close at the low on heavy volume. On the third day down the decline had exceeded 60 points, and "the main trend had turned, both on time and points".
- **Buy panicky declines, sell rapid advances (p. 200). Time balance (p. 205):** a four-month decline against a four-month advance: "Time had balanced."
- **Bull market sections, cotton 1921–23 (p. 205).** Three sections are measured. The fourth month of a fast advance ends it, and "fast advances seldom run more than four months".
- **Cotton move-length statistics, 47 years (p. 218).** Moves of ≤1 month: 102; 2 months: 52; 3: 27; 4: 16; 5: 12; 6: 7; 7: 0; 8: 2; 9: 3; 10: 2.
  - Fast moves rarely exceed 4 months.
  - **Long swings seldom last more than 3 years before a ~12-month reaction.** A decline into its third year seldom passes 36 months before a 9–12-month advance (p. 219).
- **Watch 1, 1½, 2, 2½, 3, 3½, 4 and 5 years** from any important top or bottom (p. 218).
- **Volume, cotton 1941 daily (pp. 220–222).** The largest sales come a few days before tops, and sales shrink on reactions in an uptrend. A record-volume panic day (Oct 16 1941, 549,000 bales) marked the low. This supports X1's panic-bottom exception.
- **Crop cycles (pp. 222–229).** The record-crop to record-crop intervals are 3–12 years. Gann forecast "the period from 1841 to 1850 is likely to be repeated in 1941 to 1950" (p. 224), a 100-year repetition stated as belief.
- **Cotton seed oil (pp. 237–241).**
  - A level that holds for days or weeks without being penetrated by more than 30 points is making a top or bottom.
  - **Stops scale with price level**: 30 points at ≤10¢ and 50 points at >20¢. This is Gann's own price-scaled buffer, relevant to porting lost motion.

### Butter, cocoa, coffee, eggs, hides, pepper, potatoes, rubber, silk, sugar, wool (pp. 241–305)
- Each gets a seasonal table, a list of extreme highs and lows, and a resistance-level table computed from the extreme high and low. The same rules are reapplied.
- **Butter (p. 244).** The most important changes come **6 and 12 months apart**. After three highs or lows in one month in three consecutive years, the next year runs opposite.
- **Cocoa (p. 250).** Watch for a change "around the fifteenth month".
- **Coffee (pp. 253–269).**
  - "Coffee comes remarkably close at the end of every three-year period" (p. 269).
  - A secondary rally after a sharp top is often only ~50% of the first decline, so double tops may not form (p. 269).
  - Numerology flag: p. 264 calls nine years "three times three, making this important". This is **not admissible** (the same class as A8 p. 58's "5th … of Ascension").
  - Gann also rejects gambling: "when in doubt, keep out" (p. 255).
- **Rubber (pp. 285–292).**
  - Bull campaigns run 2–4 sections, so watch the third.
  - "**Any time the market cannot rally into the second month, it is weak**" (p. 291).
  - Every extreme came "against some mathematical point or percentage of some former high or low price" (p. 289).
- **Silk (pp. 292–297).** "The first thing … is to find the **time cycles** because time periods are the most important."
  - Add a prior swing's duration to the extreme low.
  - Use **12½/25/50/75/100% of time periods**. Example: ⅛ of a 64-month swing = 8 months, which marked the Aug 1940 low.
  - Watch 12, 15, 18, 21, 24 months, and "**fast moves up often come in the eighth and fourth months**".
- **Sugar and wool (pp. 297–305).** Base-percentage ladders on the extreme low: 60 points → 120, 180, 240, 300 marked tops and bottoms 1932–41. Wool tracks cotton's timing.
- **1941 Conclusion (p. 306):** study, prove the rules to yourself, "the hard way is deep study and hard work."

### The 1951 "New Rules for Trading in Commodities" (pp. 307–328)
This is the section flagged as the highest-priority gap. It **settles conflict X3** (see below).

1. **Fast moves correct position in short time periods (p. 308).** A rapid, above-normal decline after an overbought advance corrects the weak technical position. Examples: May soy beans fell 116¼¢ in 30 days in 1948, and 46¢ in 12 days in 1949. **A secondary rally nearly always follows, and many months pass before the low is broken.** The mirror applies to oversold markets.
2. **Time turns trend (pp. 309–310).** The **"TIME TREND TURN"** is defined by days, not cents:
   - after a final top, a swing down of one, two, three days or more
   - then a swing up making a **lower top**
   - then a decline that **breaks the low of the first swing down**
   - At a bottom the mirror applies: crossing the rally high that preceded a higher bottom.
   - **Exception:** a sharp single top with no time turn. There, use gaps, reverse signal days, resistance levels and limit days.
3. **Normal price moves (p. 310).** **Normal move = 1/16 of the price level**: 6¼¢ at $1, 12½¢ at $2, 18¾¢ at $3. Beyond that, watch 37½¢.
   - This is Gann's own **price-scaled** normal swing: an explicit precedent for porting point thresholds by price level rather than literally (relevant to the X3 9-point chart and to lost motion).
4. **Normal time moves (pp. 310–311).**
   - A normal reaction in a steady advance lasts **10–14 days**. If exceeded, the next is **28–30 days**.
   - **A move exceeding 30 days is likely to run 60 days.**
   - **The close matters.** A new high on the 14th day that closes near the low is not continuation until the price crosses that high and closes well above it.
5. **Time periods and balancing of time (pp. 311–312).**
   - Measure top→top, bottom→bottom, bottom→top and top→bottom. When the current move equals a prior one, time is balanced.
   - "**The longer the period of time that elapses before a previous high level is crossed … the greater the advance.**" After the 1925 wheat high was crossed in 1947, it ran about +50% of that high (205⅞ → 306⅞).
   - **Weekly counts from extremes: 6½–7, 13, 17, 20, 26, 32½, 35, 42–45, 52 weeks.** **"All the ANNIVERSARY DATES … one year from any extreme high or low are the most important."**
6. **Price swings overbalanced (p. 312).** The first reaction exceeding the previous down-swing (in price) indicates change. **"Never consider that the trend has definitely changed until the TIME PERIOD has OVERBALANCED."** Example: reactions of 5–7 days, then a down-swing of more than 7 days.
   - **Both price and time overbalanced means a definite change.**
   - **"All rules work best when markets are very active and near extreme high or extreme low levels."** Reversals in mid-range mean less. Use all the rules together.
7. **Prices to use (pp. 312–315).**
   - **The extreme high and low of the traded contract are the most important**, and cash extremes are secondary.
   - Use percentages of both, with secondary tops and bottoms of secondary importance.
   - Worked (p. 313): 67¢ × 550% + 67 = 435¾ against a 436¾ top. ½ and ¾ of 67–436¾ gave the 1949 low and 1951 high.
   - **"½ of any price or 50% decline or advance is most important"** (p. 314).
   - **Anniversary:** the Feb 8 1951 high came one year after the Feb 6 1950 low.
8. **Important news causes trend changes (p. 315).** The extreme just before sudden news (Pearl Harbor, 7 Dec 1941; Korea, 26 Jun 1950) becomes a new **origin for time counts and resistance levels**.
   - **Balancing point:** add the first advance from that origin to its high (209⅝ + 62⅝ → 272, projecting 334¾; actual high 334).
9. **Swing trading is most profitable (p. 316).** Hold until a reverse swing, and don't scalp. The two 1950 soy bean swings ran $1.02 and $1.10 with no swing bottom broken and no reaction over 3 and 5 days respectively.
10. **SWING CHARTS (p. 316): the definition.**
    - "The best kind to keep for daily moves is a chart which **records all reverse moves or reactions that run 2 to 3 days**."
    - After a series of up-swings, **the first time prices break below the last SWING BOTTOM on the 2 or 3-day chart, the trend is changing**.
    - In a decline, the first cross of the last swing high signals the change.
11. **WEEKLY SWING CHARTS (pp. 316–317).** "A Swing Chart which records **reverse moves of 7 calendar days or more** is of greater importance."
    - The line moves only when a counter-move lasts 7 days or more.
    - After a 4-week advance, a reaction of 7 days or more moves the chart down to the bottom of the reaction.
    - Later, **breaking the low of the previous down-swing** signals the change.
    - "**I consider the 7-DAY SWING CHART one of the most valuable trend indicators.**"
    - Worked example: Nov soy beans 1950, where the first down-swing longer than 4 days followed four up-swings with no reverse move over 4 days.
12. **Three rules for extreme prices (pp. 317–325).** In very active markets use the daily chart and apply the three together.
    - **Reverse signal day:**
      - opens above the prior high, makes a new high, closes near the low (mirror at bottoms)
      - or, after 7–10 (up to 14) days without breaking a prior day's low, the first day that does and closes near the low
      - This is the **"7 to 10 Day Rule"**.
    - **Gaps:**
      - upside and downside gaps, caused by overnight news
      - an **exhaust gap** is a one-day gap at an extreme, filled the next day, often near a ½ point. Put the stop 1¢ beyond the gap day's extreme and reverse when it is hit.
      - **3–4 consecutive gaps in new territory** means a culmination is near (6–7 counting inside gaps, up to 10 in extremes)
      - after an exhaust gap, a 3-day reverse move indicates change
      - **inside gaps** matter less
      - **gaps between an old and a new contract** matter more for the long pull and are usually filled only after a further move away
      - **a filled gap reverses the minor trend only**
    - **Limit days:** two consecutive limit moves closing at the extreme means selling (or buying) at the next open with a 2–3¢ stop. A limit day plus a gap plus a reverse signal at a ½ point is the strongest top signal, then confirmed by over-balanced price and time.
13. **Long-term investing in futures (pp. 325–326).** A spread technique for tax-period management. Out of scope for GSPS.
14. **Conclusion (pp. 326–328):** "apply mathematical scientific rules and gain Knowledge … before they risk their money."

### Statistical appendix (pp. 367–408)
- Data tables only, useful as a historical dataset rather than method.
- **Crop-record prose (pp. 399–403).** Gann logs each crop as "one/two/three years larger" or "smaller" than the prior, and the interval from a record crop to the shortest crop: wheat 10 years (1915→1925) and 19 years (1915→1934); corn 5–7-year runs.
  - He expects "1942 to 1944 the record should be broken" from these runs. This is crop-cycle forecasting by counting runs, the same run-counting logic as `ruleOfThree.ts` applied to annual data.
  - It is not portable to equities.

## X3 — what the 1951 New Rules settle
- **Gann's own swing charts, across three texts, are defined by the *duration of counter-moves*, measured on the bar's highs and lows, not by runs of closes:**
  - **A8 1951, p. 316:** the daily chart records reverse moves of **2 to 3 days**. The trend turns when price breaks the **last swing bottom** (or crosses the last swing top).
  - **A8 1951, pp. 316–317:** the weekly chart records reverse moves of **7 calendar days or more**, with the same break-of-swing rule.
  - **A8 1941, p. 200:** a 30-point chart and a "reactions never exceed 2 days" rule for fast markets.
  - **A09 1949 Ch. VII:** a 3-day chart on highs and lows, and a **9-point** chart.
- **What `lib/gann/swingChart.ts` does.** It flips on N consecutive *opposing closes* (3 and 9), and its direction is the latest flip. That differs from Gann's rules in three ways:
  - (a) Gann counts **days of the counter-move**, not consecutive opposing closes. A 3-day reaction may include an up-close.
  - (b) **The trend changes when the prior swing extreme is broken**, not when the counter-move ends. The counter-move only moves the chart; the break of the last swing bottom or top is the signal.
  - (c) **Gann's longer chart is time-based at 7 calendar days** (1951) **or price-based at 9 points** (1949). **Neither is "9 days".**
- **Reading for the owner.**
  - The closest literal translation is a **2–3-day swing chart** (counter-moves of ≥2–3 bars move the line; the trend flips on breaking the last swing low or high) plus a **7-calendar-day chart** (counter-moves of ≥7 calendar days, ≈5 sessions).
  - The **9-point** chart would need a price-scaled threshold. Gann's own 1951 "normal move = 1/16 of price" (p. 310) and the cotton-seed-oil stops scaled by price level (p. 239) are the documented precedent for that scaling.
- **Who depends on it:** `swingChartTrend` (scored), `trendStrength.ts` (regime) and `readGannTrend` (macro direction).
- **Status: owner decision. Not changed here.** It moves the core verdict, and the 6/3.5 cutoffs would need re-deriving.
- **Note also:** the 1951 **"time trend turn"** (lower top, then a break of the first swing low) is the same structure `trendStrength.ts` already reads as "stepping swings". That part of the current design *is* Gann-aligned; the swing construction underneath is what differs.

## p. 52 (second scan, 2026-09-27) — sideways movements and accumulation on the side
A second Drive upload of the same Lambert 1976 printing (217 two-page spreads, image-only, OCR'd locally) supplied p. 52, the one page missing from the first scan. The rest of that copy matches the first scan. Its appendix ends on p. 412 (potato acreage, production and trade, 1911–1940), a data table like pp. 367–408.
- **Sideways movements.** Commodities don't only go up or down. They often hold for weeks or months in a range, making neither a new top nor a new bottom. **Leave the market alone until it breaks out** into new high or new low ground. Then the trend is known with some certainty and a close stop is possible. Trading inside the range gives smaller chances. Sideways moves are "periods of rest and preparation" for the next move.
- **Distribution on the side.** After a campaign reaches its **3rd or 4th section**, a sharp decline and a rally can be followed by a long range *below* the extreme high while distribution happens. It is a short sale at the top of that range, and **safer when the range's low breaks: the "breakaway point."**
- **Accumulation on the side** is the mirror at the end of a bear campaign. After the first sharp advance and a secondary reaction, price ranges for a long time. It is a buy at the range bottom, and **safer when the range tops are crossed (the breakaway point), which signals a fast advance.**
- **Last stage of a campaign:** in the final section, **reactions get smaller** as prices rise (rallies get smaller as prices fall in a bear market), until the final run ends in a sharp, quick reversal. Don't buck the trend in the last stage.
- This page is the commodity statement of rules found across the stock books: A4 Ch. VII ("the more time consumed in accumulation, the bigger the advance"; buy after the stock crosses the zone of accumulation) and A4 Ch. V (wait for 3 points beyond the old top or bottom of a sideways move).
- **GSPS cross-reference:** the range-then-breakaway rule is what `lib/signals/states/rangeReversion.ts` and `lib/gann/entryTrigger.ts` split between them. Gann's own preference is explicit: trade the **breakaway**, not the inside of the range; buying the range bottom is allowed, but the breakout is "safer". The "reactions shrink in the last section" rule adds to gap G22 (time/price balance) as an exhaustion signal.
